    Foreword
    The ability to decide freely and responsibly whether, when, and how many children to have is a
    cornerstone of reproductive rights and bodily autonomy. Access to quality contraceptive information
    and services empowers individuals—particularly women and girls—to safeguard their health, pursue
    education and employment, and shape their futures on their own terms. When these choices are
    supported, the benefits extend beyond individuals to families, communities, and entire societies through
    improved health, gender equality, and economic growth.

    While significant progress has been made globally in expanding contraceptive access, critical gaps
    persist. As of 2021, 164 million of the 1.1 billion women of reproductive age with a need for pregnancy
    prevention were not using any contraceptive method. These shortfalls are not only the result of health
    system and supply chain limitations—they also reflect deeply rooted social and behavioural factors.
    Norms, beliefs, misinformation, partner and community dynamics, provider attitudes, and structural
    inequities all profoundly shape contraceptive decision-making and use.

    Social and behavior change (SBC) approaches offer powerful tools to address these barriers. By
    working across individual, interpersonal, community, and systemic levels, SBC interventions help create
    enabling environments where people can make informed, voluntary decisions about contraception.
    From improving provider-client interactions and countering stigma to shifting harmful gender norms
    and strengthening accountability, SBC strategies are essential complements to service delivery efforts.

    Despite their proven value, SBC interventions in contraception have often been underutilized,
    underfunded, and insufficiently guided by evidence. As a result, their potential has not been fully
    realized. Policymakers and programme implementers have lacked clear, evidence-based guidance on
    how to design, deliver, evaluate, and scale SBC interventions in ways that are both contextually relevant
    and aligned with health system goals.

    It is with great commitment and urgency that the World Health Organization presents its first-ever
    guidance on social and behavior change interventions for contraception and family planning. This
    guidance provides a practical and evidence-informed roadmap to integrate SBC into national
    contraceptive strategies. It equips decision-makers and programme managers with the tools to
    advocate for, invest in, and implement SBC approaches that can drive meaningful change.

    Grounded in WHO’s commitment to rights-based, people-centered health, this guidance emphasizes
    equity, community engagement, and the use of data throughout the programme cycle. When adopted
    and applied, it can help countries accelerate progress toward their family planning goals and ensure
    that every person—regardless of circumstance—can realize their reproductive intentions and exercise
    their rights.

    Investing in SBC is not a supplementary measure—it is a strategic imperative. This guidance is intended
    to support all stakeholders in translating commitments into impact, helping to build a future where
    informed reproductive choice is a universal reality.

    Pascale Allotey (PhD)
    Director SRH & HRP

Glossary of terms
The following terms are used specifically for the purposes of this guide.

Social and behaviour change (SBC): SBC is defined as an approach which seeks to understand and
address the overlapping individual, social and environmental factors that influence behaviours to
produce the behavioural and social change necessary for improved health and wellbeing.

SBC intervention: SBC interventions use evidence-based approaches to reduce barriers and support
enablers to achieve desired behavioural and social change. They aim to empower people to act on
their intentions, enabling the fulfilment of individuals’ health and rights.

Scale for SBC interventions: Scale for SBC interventions is defined as the deliberate, ethically grounded
and participatory process of expanding the reach and impact of effective and sustainable SBC
interventions to benefit more people and foster lasting change for positive health and social outcomes.
The effectiveness and sustainability of interventions should be determined by impacted communities.

Defining social and behaviour
change


  Key Takeaways:
  •   SBC is an approach that addresses individual, interpersonal,
      social and environmental factors influencing health
      behaviours and social phenomena. It uses evidence-driven
      theories from social and behavioural sciences to design
      impactful interventions.

  •   Behaviours and social processes are influenced by multiple
      factors at different levels, as described by the socioecological
      model (SEM) and capability, opportunity motivation,
      and behaviour (COM-B) framework. These models help
      identify specific barriers and enablers to address with SBC
      interventions.

  •   SBC interventions go beyond raising awareness of family
      planning and contraceptives. They aim to change the
      physical and social environment to make it easier for people
      to adopt and sustain positive reproductive health behaviours.
      Interventions should address system-wide barriers, not just
      individual knowledge and attitudes.

  •   SBC interventions must be human rights-based, gender-
      responsive and people-centred. Following these principles
      helps to ensure interventions respect individuals’ rights and
      promote voluntarism, address gender inequities and involve
      communities in the design process.

  •   SBC interventions in contraceptive and family planning
      programming should focus on equity and inclusion,
      particularly for vulnerable and underserved populations
      such as adolescents, people with disabilities and those in
      humanitarian settings.

      affordability, accessibility and reliability of contraceptive services. SBC
      contributes to all six WHO health system building blocks, from service
      delivery to leadership and governance.

            Key Takeaways:
             •   SBC interventions for contraceptive programming should
                 address barriers and enablers at multiple levels of the
                 socioecological model, including structural/institutional,
                 social, interpersonal and individual factors. Effective
                 interventions often combine approaches to address multiple
                 levels simultaneously.

             •   Key factors influencing contraceptive and family
                 planning behaviours and social norms operate at
                 various levels: structural/institutional (e.g., policies and
                 health system capacity), social (e.g., cultural norms and
                 stigma), interpersonal (e.g., partner influence and family
                 expectations) and individual (e.g., knowledge, attitudes and
                 self-efficacy).

             •   Evidence supports the effectiveness of various SBC
                 interventions in achieving contraceptive and family planning
                 outcomes, including community engagement, provider
                 behaviour change strategies, mass media campaigns and
                 digital health interventions. However, the impact of these
                 interventions is highly context-dependent, emphasizing the
                 need for tailored, evidence-based design.

             •   Adapting SBC interventions for specific populations is
                 crucial for maximizing effectiveness and ensuring equity.
                 Key populations to consider within contraceptive and family
                 planning programming include adolescents, men and boys,
                 postpartum women, postabortion women and vulnerable

    groups such as people living with HIV or in fragile settings.

•   For adolescents, effective interventions often combine service
    delivery improvements, health worker training, demand-
    generation activities and community engagement. These
    interventions should actively involve adolescents in the
    planning process.

•   Engaging men and boys in contraceptive programming
    requires a comprehensive approach that addresses gender
    norms, promotes shared decision-making and recognizes
    men both as supporters of their partners and as contraceptive
    users themselves.

•   For postpartum women, effective SBC interventions include
    integrating family planning services into postnatal care
    and leveraging antenatal care and immunization visits
    for counselling and engaging partners. Home visits and
    community group engagement have proven especially
    impactful, particularly for young married women and
    adolescents.

•   Postabortion care provides a crucial opportunity for
    contraceptive counselling and services. Effective
    interventions include offering information and services at the
    same place and time as facility-based postabortion care,
    addressing stigma through community engagement and
    ensuring provider competence in family planning counselling
    for this specific group.

•   Significant gaps in the evidence base remain, particularly
    regarding effective SBC interventions for people living with
    disabilities, especially in fragile settings. More rigorous impact
    evidence is needed on SBC interventions in humanitarian
    contexts, and there is a lack of studies on interventions
    aimed at delaying first pregnancies among young couples
    compared to spacing later pregnancies.

                                          the sexual and reproductive health (SRH) and wellbeing of adolescents, including reducing

                                          services (66).

                                          The most effective adolescent sexual and reproductive health services combine facility-
                                          based approaches with community acceptance and demand-generation activities (66).


 Task shifting                            A cohort study compared how pharmacists versus clinicians prescribe hormonal
                                          contraception in four states in the United States (67). Providing more than a 1-month supply is

                                          pharmacist prescription of hormonal contraception was associated with a contraceptive
                                          supply of more than 6 months dispensed compared with traditional clinic-based
                                          prescriptions. The women who received contraception from pharmacists were younger, had
                                          less education and were more likely to be uninsured than women seeing clinicians.


 Supportive policy environment            A study in the United States compared oral contraceptive pill continuation rates between
                                          women who obtained pills over-the-counter in Mexican pharmacies and those who received
                                          them by prescription from U.S. family planning clinics. The results showed lower continuation
                                          rates among clinic users, especially those receiving fewer pill packs (69).

                                          These types of policy-level interventions, such as removing prescription requirements for
                                          oral contraceptives and allowing over-the-counter access or private sector distribution, hold
                                          significant potential to improve access and uptake of contraception, especially in countries
                                          with high unmet needs. By expanding contraceptive options and enhancing availability,
                                          these strategies can play a key role in supporting public health efforts and advancing
                                          contraceptive coverage (70).

                                          provider motivation, efficiency and empathy to improve quality of care (62,64,71,72). This can

                                          and/or coaching/mentoring and financial incentives (44,73).

            Key Takeaways:
            •    A structured five-step process (define, diagnose, design, implement
                 and evaluate) can be used for designing and implementing evidence-
                 based SBC interventions to support contraceptive and family planning
                 programming. This approach ensures interventions are tailored to
                 specific contexts and address identified social and behavioural barriers
                 and enablers.

            •    Engaging affected communities and stakeholders is crucial throughout
                 the SBC process, from defining the challenge to evaluating outcomes.
                 This participatory approach ensures interventions are culturally
                 appropriate, feasible and address the actual needs of the affected
                 population.

            •    Behavioural frameworks like the SEM and COM-B help systematically
                 identify and address factors influencing reproductive health behaviours
                 and social processes. This structured approach aids in designing more
                 targeted and potentially effective interventions.

            •    Developing a theory of change is essential for designing and evaluating
                 SBC interventions in contraceptive and family planning programming.
                 Theories of change provide a clear roadmap linking activities to
                 outputs, outcomes and ultimate health impacts, facilitating more
                 rigorous evaluation and learning.

            •    Iteration and adaptation are important at each stage of the SBC
                 process. This flexible approach allows for continuous improvement
                 based on new insights, feedback and small-scale testing results,
                 enhancing the likelihood of intervention success.

    A process for monitoring and
    evaluating SBC interventions


            Key Takeaways:
             •   Monitoring, evaluating and learning for SBC interventions in
                 contraceptive programming involves a comprehensive process of
                 testing, monitoring and evaluating interventions to assess effectiveness
                 and impact. This process is guided by the intervention’s theory of
                 change and includes both quantitative and qualitative methods.

             •   Testing SBC interventions at a small scale before full implementation
                 is crucial for assessing feasibility, identifying challenges and refining
                 strategies. Implementation research during this phase helps bridge the
                 gap between what is known to be effective and what is implemented in
                 practice.

             •   Monitoring SBC interventions involves the ongoing assessment of
                 activities, outputs and outcomes. Key data to collect include reach,
                 costs, fidelity and outputs. Effective monitoring allows for timely
                 adjustments and improvements to the intervention.

             •   Evaluating SBC interventions should measure both social and
                 behavioural outcomes and the intermediate outcomes (drivers) that
                 influence these changes. This approach helps understand not just if an
                 intervention worked, but how and why it was effective or ineffective.

             •   Key considerations for evaluating SBC interventions include using
                 appropriate study designs, considering timeframes for change, using
                 multiple approaches to understand causality, assessing unintended
                 consequences and advocating for community-led monitoring to ensure
                 interventions are rooted in local context and needs.

  Key Takeaways:
  •   Scaling effective SBC interventions in contraceptive programming is
      crucial for maximizing the impact on reproductive health outcomes.
      By reaching more people and fostering lasting change, scaled
      interventions can lead to significant reductions in unintended
      pregnancies, unsafe abortions and maternal mortality rates, while also
      becoming more cost-effective and sustainable over time.

  •   Implementing SBC interventions at scale can complement investments
      in contraceptive supply and delivery by ensuring individuals are
      empowered to make informed choices about contraceptive uptake. This
      complementary approach helps countries achieve their reproductive
      health goals, such as FP2030 commitments, by addressing a full range
      of factors influencing contraceptive use.

  •   Scaling up SBC interventions for contraceptive programming involves a
      deliberate, ethically grounded and participatory process of expanding
      effective and sustainable interventions as determined by the impacted
      community.

  •   The WHO ExpandNet framework can be adapted for SBC interventions to
      provide a structured approach to scaling up.

  •   Before scaling, SBC interventions for contraceptive and family planning
      should be assessed for readiness using criteria such as credibility,
      observability, relevance, relative advantage, ease of implementation,
      compatibility with existing systems and testability. These criteria help
      ensure the intervention is appropriate and likely to succeed at scale.

  •   Creating an enabling environment for scaling SBC interventions is
      crucial. Key factors include securing sufficient financial and human
      resources, ensuring appropriate and contextualised intervention design,
      building strong partnerships for sustainability and implementing
      effective monitoring and evaluation systems.

    Case study: Reducing adolescent pregnancy through national initiatives


                                                             Type: Programme evaluation

                                                             Location: Chile, England, Ethiopia
            Target population: Adolescents


Initiatives to reduce adolescent pregnancy in Chile, England and Ethiopia have varied in their approaches
but share commonalities in their focus on designing for scale from the outset through effective government
coordination, multisectoral collaboration and using data-driven management, thereby setting a framework
that could inspire similar efforts in other regions.

  Chile: Achieved a 51% reduction in births to mothers under 19 from 2000 to 2017. The strategy included
  comprehensive health programmes and legal reforms to protect youth. The programme's success was
  supported by data-driven management and multi-sector collaboration.

  England: Saw a 55% reduction in the under-18 conception rate from 1998 to 2015. The strategy involved
  a multi-agency approach and strong advocacy, focusing on integrating sexual health education and
  access to contraception.

  Ethiopia: Reported increases of 29% and 38% in contraceptive use among married adolescent girls from
  2000 to 2016. The government implemented the Health Extension Programme, providing community-based
  health education and services.

Key strategies from these three countries to design for scale included the following:

 Strong government partnerships: Effective leadership and ownership at the national level were crucial,
 guiding the strategic direction and ensuring sustained commitment across political cycles.

 Multisectoral collaboration: Successful strategies involved collaboration across multiple sectors, including
 health, education and social services.

 Focus on institutionalisation: Integrating programmes into broader national policies and ongoing health
 systems ensured long-term impact and resource allocation.

 Data-driven management: Regular monitoring and the use of data to assess progress and make
 adjustments were integral to each programme's success (200).


                Case study: Scaling up family planning in urban Kenya

The Tupange Project, part of the Urban Reproductive Health Initiative funded by the Bill and Melinda Gates
Foundation, aimed to enhance family planning services in Kenya from 2010 to 2015. Initially focused on the
expansion cities of Machakos and Kakamega, the project utilized the ExpandNet approach for scalable and
sustainable intervention strategies. Successes in these areas led to the application of learned strategies
in the larger core cities of Nairobi, Kisumu and Mombasa. The project emphasized government ownership,
integrated interventions within existing health systems and prioritized sustainability. The approach adapted
to the unique local needs and leveraged local resources to broaden the impact, facilitating a successful
scale-up in urban settings (201).
