## Source PDF page 9

Each time she crossed steep mountain slopes in the freezing night to deliver a baby,
physician Lò Thị Thanh would quietly remind herself: “If I give up too, how can I expect the
community to change?”
Only three days into her assignment at the Mu Sang Commune Health Station (Phong
Tho District, Lai Chau Province), physician Lò Thị Thanh (46, originally from Dien Bien) was
already called into the village for a delivery. It was a critical case-a mother with a retained
placenta.
“The roads weren’t paved back then. It was all steep, slippery slopes. Her family had to ride
a motorbike out to get me,” Thanh recalls vividly of that day in 2007. The motorbike skidded
down the slope, as if it were plunging into a ravine. When she finally arrived, Thanh exhaled
deeply and whispered with trembling voice: “Mother, I’m still alive.”
In Mu Sang, many women still choose to give birth at home. To them, childbirth is strictly a
matter between women - a domestic affair, with no need for health workers. They believe
that if a baby is born where their mother once gave birth, that child will enter the world
safely too.
But thanks to Thanh’s unwavering commitment, that mindset is slowly shifting. Women
who once hesitated at the sight of a white coat now call out confidently, “Ms. Thanh, I’m
having contractions.” Husbands, who once saw childbirth as “women’s business,” now sit
quietly outside the commune clinic, waiting for their wives to give birth.

Each time she sat down with a hesitant husband or a skeptical village elder to talk
about childbirth, village-based birth attendant Hnhach quietly reminded herself: “If I
don’t speak up, who will protect our mothers?”
In the remote De Ar commune of Gia Lai province, Ba Na women have given birth at
home for generations, relying on the untrained hands of family members. “I gave birth
to six children, five of them at home. Who assisted me? My husband,” recalls Ms. Yeng,
a local mother. To them, the clinic simply felt too far, the travel too expensive, and the
hospital too unfamiliar.

## Source PDF page 12

Hnhach knew that changing these deeply
ingrained cultural norms would be no
small task. After completing an intensive
midwifery training supported by UNFPA
and MSD for Mothers, she returned to
her village with a mission. She knocked
on doors, having difficult conversations
not just with expectant mothers, but with
husbands, mothers-in-law, and village
chiefs to advocate for hospital deliveries.
For the women who still felt too afraid
or financially constrained to make the
journey, Hnhach became their lifeline. In 2022 alone, she safely assisted 24 home births, bringing
medical expertise directly to their bedsides.
But thanks to Hnhach’s gentle persistence, the mindset in De Ar is shifting. Expectant mothers are
letting go of their fears and embracing clinic check-ups. “I am so grateful to Ms. Hnhach,” shared
Ms. Bloy, a local mother. “She didn’t just help me; she advised me to prioritize my health.”
“If I don’t speak up, who will protect our mothers?” - Today, Hnhach is living proof
that with unwavering dedication, anyone can break through the barriers of tradition to rewrite
the future of their community, one safe delivery at a time.
