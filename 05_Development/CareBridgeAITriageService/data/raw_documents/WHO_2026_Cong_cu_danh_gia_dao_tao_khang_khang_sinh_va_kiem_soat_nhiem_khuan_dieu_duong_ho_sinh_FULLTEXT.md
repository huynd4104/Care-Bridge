1. Introduction
AMR occurs when bacteria, viruses, fungi and parasites change over time and do not respond
to antimicrobial medicines, making infections more difficult to treat and increasing the risk of
disease spread, severe illness and death. AMR also increases the cost of treatment, saturates
scarce health-care capacity and decreases trust in health care (9).
AMR is a health and cross-cutting development issue, with drivers in multiple sectors, of which
poor IPC is the main one (10). AMR and IPC are thus interdependent global health priorities that
significantly impact patient safety and health system resilience. Inadequate IPC measures, such
as poor hand hygiene, unsafe injection practices and insufficient water, sanitation and hygiene
infrastructure, increase susceptibility to health-care-associated infections (HAIs) and the spread
of AMR. HAIs are a major contributor to morbidity, mortality and health-care costs worldwide,
reinforcing the need for strong IPC systems to protect patients and health-care workers alike and
strengthen occupational health and safety.
Key targets in the political declaration of the second United Nations General
Assembly High-level Meeting on AMR (2024)
• Scale up action to reduce the global number of deaths associated with bacterial AMR by
10% by 2030 from the 4.95 million deaths in 2019.
• All countries have basic water, sanitation, hygiene and waste services in all healthcare facilities, 90% of countries meet WHO minimum requirements for national IPC
programmes by 2030.
• Use of WHO Access group antibiotics (23) constitutes 70% of all human antibiotic use
globally by 2030.
One of the most important approaches to addressing AMR and IPC is to ensure that health workers
acquire, in their education and training, the necessary competence to prevent and mitigate AMR
and HAIs. The Global Action Plan on AMR (11), which is being updated, identifies education on
and awareness of AMR as key elements for strengthening understanding of AMR by all healthcare professionals at all levels of the health system. The report of the United Nations Interagency
Coordination Group on AMR (12), the WHO people-centred approach to addressing AMR in
human health (13) and the political declaration of the second United Nations General Assembly
High-level Meeting on AMR (14), as well as regional strategies and policies, commit countries to
promote awareness on AMR and skill in appropriate use and disposal of antimicrobials through
continuous education and training. Similarly, the Global action plan and monitoring framework
for IPC for 2024–2030 (15) lists key elements of national curricula on IPC and the importance of
education and training in IPC for all health and care workers, with the goal of a national curriculum
for IPC professionals and a training programme for health and care workers in more than 80% of
countries2 Antimicrobial resistance and infection prevention and control

In 2018, WHO published a Competency framework for health workers’ education and training
on antimicrobial resistance (16) for academic institutions and regulatory agencies to ensure
that the pre-service and in-service training they provide equip health workers with adequate
competence to address AMR. This publication was followed by Health workers’ training and
education on AMR: curriculum guide (17) to ensure the inclusion of quality control and standards
to improve the competence of health workers in AMR-sensitive and -specific issues. In 2024, WHO
published the AMR curriculum assessment tool for medical education (18) to provide guidance to
medical schools and universities on AMR-specific and -sensitive curricular elements (19). Another
important normative document is Infection prevention and control pre-service education and
training curriculum: foundational content for all health and care workers (20), which defines the
essential knowledge, skills and behavioural competence necessary for delivering safe health care
and for reducing the prevalence of HAIs.
The tool is designed specifically to assess pre-service curricula for nurses and midwives for
inclusion of AMR and IPC. By identifying gaps and strengths in curricula, the tool supports
targeted improvements to ensure that graduates are adequately competent in AMR and IPC in
clinical settings. When combined with normative documents, such as the WHO guidelines on core
components of infection prevention and control programmes at the national and acute health
care facility level (21) and Antimicrobial stewardship programmes in health-care facilities in lowand middle-income countries: a WHO practical toolkit (22), this approach adds to a continuum of
quality improvement, linking education to practice and measurable progress towards global AMR
containment and IPC objectives.
This AMR and IPC curriculum assessment tool for nursing and midwifery education provides a
method for conducting a rapid, comprehensive assessment of nursing and midwifery curricula
for pre-service and postgraduate education in national universities and nursing schools to
evaluate their alignment with recommended AMR- and IPC-related educational content. The tool
is applicable to institutions that provide 3- or 4-year bachelor’s degree programmes in nursing
and/or midwifery and post-graduate specialist training. It could also be adapted for used in inservice training programmes for nurses and midwives who are already working in health-care
facilities. Although the tool is not designed for assessing curricula for training nurse technicians
and auxiliary nurses, the core principles and competencies outlined could be adapted to review
diploma or certificate-level programmes where appropriate.
2. Purpose3 Antimicrobial resistance and infection prevention and control

The objectives of curriculum assessment are to:
• facilitate rapid assessment of the curricula for education and training of nursing and midwifery
students in nursing and midwifery schools, universities or a national context, for integration of
AMR and IPC, including evaluation of the alignment of teaching and learning content with the
core competencies listed in the WHO competency framework for health workers’ education
and training on antimicrobial resistance (17) and the WHO IPC pre-service education and
training curriculum: foundational content for all health and care workers (18);
• support curriculum strengthening by informing a strategy or reviewing existing processes and
structures to enhance the AMR and IPC content of the curriculum for education and training
of nursing and midwifery students in a university or national context; and
• promote structured dialogue and continuous improvement by stimulating AMR-and IPC-related
competencies in nursing and midwifery faculties in universities and relevant stakeholders
such as nursing councils and regulatory and accreditation bodies.
3. Objectives
The first draft of the document was prepared by the AMR department, with inputs from various
other WHO headquarters teams, including Health Workforce, Infection Prevention and Control
and Office of the Chief Nursing Officer. Structured input was also collected from WHO Regional
Offices. Nine external global experts in AMR and nursing education were recruited according to
due process and regional representation, and the draft tool was revised with their feedback. The
revised tool was pilot tested in 3 nursing schools in Zimbabwe, and qualitative insights gathered
during piloting were used for improving the contents of the tool. The tool was peer-reviewed by
three independent experts.
4. Approach to development4 Antimicrobial resistance and infection prevention and control
Curriculum assessment tool for nursing and midwifery education
A curriculum assessment is not a one-time activity but is the beginning of a process of strengthening
the capacity of a nursing and or midwifery school or university to deliver education and training
in AMR and IPC, including water, sanitation and hygiene. It should begin with full support and
agreement from the senior management of the school, college, university, regulatory agency for
nursing and midwifery education or the relevant ministry, whichever is applicable.
A curriculum assessment is a three-step process (Fig. 1], starting with a desk review to collect all
the necessary published documents and materials on the nursing curriculum and syllabi. At the
end of the desk review, a draft report is circulated to relevant faculty members. This is followed
up by an online or in-person workshop, whichever is convenient, although in-person workshops
are recommended for the initial assessment to ensure detailed mapping and consensus building.
The purpose of the workshop is to confirm and validate the report and discuss an action plan
to strengthen the AMR and IPC elements of the curriculum. All three steps are facilitated by the
curriculum assessment tool: the information required to populate the tool is collected during the
desk review; the draft report is based on entries to the tool; and the workshop participants go
through the tool together and confirm the responses.
Fig. 1. Curriculum assessment
Desk review
The rapid curriculum assessment should start with a desk review, for which a focal person collects
data from curriculum documents, course outlines and detailed syllabi of courses given at the
university, to identify any gaps and areas that should be modified. This can be completed in
approximate 10–20 person-hours, depending on curriculum organization.

Workshop in person or online
The suggested duration of the workshop is half a day for an in-person format and 3 h if held
online. In either case, provision should be made for a breakout discussion among representatives
of each department to arrive at a consensus on the scores.
Ideally, the workshop should be attended by representatives of the following departments and
specialities and the course coordinator:
• medical and surgical nursing,
• paediatric nursing,
• obstetric (or perinatal) nursing,
• infection prevention and control,
• community health and/or public and/or global health nursing,
• microbiology,
• pharmacology or pharmacy,
• infectious diseases (when available),
• trauma and emergency nursing (when available),
• critical or intensive care nursing (when available),
• nursing research (when available),
• nursing education (when available) and

• introductory session to set the objectives of the assessment and for using the information;

• a break-out discussion among representatives of departments to deliberate on the themes

• reviewing and populating part 3 of the tool (institutional readiness assessment) as a group.
• The findings of the workshop should be documented and an action plan developed to

as applicable.
• A follow-up meeting should be held 6 months later to assess progress in following the action plan.

Curriculum assessment
Address gaps in AMR and IPC curricula identified during the assessment in departmental and
institution-wide actions. All departments should look critically at the clinical learning environment
of students, the supervision standards for AMR-, AMS- and IPC-related behaviour during clinical
rotations and the training of clinical preceptors. Each department should be able to contribute to
a brief institutional remedial plan that includes:
• specific and intended learning outcomes associated with the identified gaps and
• alignment with existing competence-based or case-based education, as applicable.
Use the WHO Competency framework for health workers’ education and training on AMR (17)
and the WHO Core competencies for infection prevention and control professionals (26) to map
gaps. For guidance on competence-based education and outcomes, please see the WHO Global
competency and outcomes framework for universal health coverage (4).
Integrated curricula should be considered to avoid compartmentalized teaching. This will
require interdepartmental courses and sessions for programme-level and institutional learning
outcomes. Curriculum mapping (27) is recommended to visualize alignment of:
• institutional learning outcomes,
• programme learning outcomes,
• course learning outcomes,
• session learning outcomes and
• assessment methods.
Prepare a curriculum map in a workshop involving all relevant departments and findings from
the curriculum assessment.
A medium-term strategy would be to integrate AMR and IPC themes into the official curriculum
recommended by the institution, university or national regulatory body and ensure clear ownership
of integrated content to prevent its dilution. Base the integration on a careful evaluation of the
AMR- and IPC-related competence expected from nurses and midwives. In settings or countries
where curricula are decided centrally, conduct assessments in several institutions and use the
results for advocacy with the agency responsible for setting curricula and syllabi.
Institutional readiness
Address the deficiencies identified in the assessment of institutional readiness as follows.
• Immediate actions: Put in place low-cost interventions such as an institutional policy for staff
training in IPC and AMR. Prepare and submit a list of immediately feasible management
interventions to leadership.
• Infrastructure and human resources: Develop a costed plan to address gaps related to
infrastructure and human resources. Include justification for prioritizing IPC and AMR, using
global, national and local data. Submit the plan to the institutional leadership for approval and
resource mobilization.
