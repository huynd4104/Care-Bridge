Facts on maternal and neonatal health in
 Belarus


Belarus is a landlocked country in eastern Europe covering an area 207 600 km2. It attained its
independence in 1991 after the break-up of the Soviet Union but has retained close political and
economic ties to Russia.1 Belarus is a republic, governed by a president and a bicameral parliament, and
the country is administratively divided into six provinces (voblasts’) and one municipality. Its economy
remains about 80% state-controlled, and agriculture is dominated by collective farming. A wide range
of redistributive policies has helped even out economic inequalities.1 Over the past decade there has
been a decrease in the population growth rate and the total population in Belarus. Cardiovascular
diseases are the leading cause of death, the second leading cause being cancer and malignant neoplasm.2

The organization of the health system has basically remained unchanged since independence. Health
care standards are set at the national level but local authorities are responsible for ensuring compliance.
The population is entitled to universal and free coverage and the health system is funded from
compulsory general taxation collected and administered by local governments.2 Primary care in rural
areas is delivered through a large network of health posts, staffed by nurses, auxiliary nurses (feldshers)
and midwives, and health stations, staffed by doctors. In urban areas, primary care is provided through
polyclinics. Primary care physicians are supposed to be the entry point to higher levels of the system
but, in practice, their gate-keeping function is not fully exercised and patients are used to referring
themselves directly to specialists.2

Both the neonatal and the perinatal mortality rates have decreased in recent years, the neonatal mortality
rate by 35% between 2000 and 2004 and the perinatal rate by 54% between 1994 and 2004. These rates
were also both below the average in the WHO European Region in 2004, by 12% for neonatal mortality
and by 39% for perinatal mortality. Given the number of live births and the mortality rate in 2004, there
were totals of 273 neonatal deaths and 513 perinatal deaths. The estimates of neonatal and perinatal
mortality rates made by WHO for 2000 suggest that these rates are being reported relatively accurately.
The reported and the estimated rates of neonatal mortality in 2000 are similar, and the estimated rate of
perinatal mortality is only slightly higher than the reported rate. This is not the case for the maternal
mortality ratio, for which the WHO estimate for 2000 was 1.42 times higher then the figure reported to
WHO for that year. The maternal mortality ratio decreased over the period 1994 to 2004 but only very
slightly, by 4.6%. The 2004 ratio of 17.99 deaths per 100 000 live births is 24% higher than the
Regional average for the same year. Given the number of live births in 2004, there were 16 maternal
deaths that year.
