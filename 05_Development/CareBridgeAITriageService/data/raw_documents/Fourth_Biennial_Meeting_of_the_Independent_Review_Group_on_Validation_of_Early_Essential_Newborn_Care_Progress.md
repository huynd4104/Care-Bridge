## Tóm tắt RAG bằng tiếng Việt

### Mục đích tài liệu
Báo cáo cuộc họp hai năm lần thứ tư của nhóm rà soát độc lập về **xác nhận tiến bộ EENC (Early Essential Newborn Care - Chăm sóc sơ sinh thiết yếu sớm)**. Tài liệu dùng khung rà soát/validation để xem xét mức triển khai và chất lượng của các thực hành EENC tại các hệ thống tham gia.

Phù hợp cho các truy vấn về **đánh giá chương trình, tiêu chí xác nhận tiến bộ, giám sát chất lượng, duy trì thực hành sau đào tạo và tổ chức cải tiến tại bệnh viện/cơ sở y tế**. Các kết quả cụ thể theo quốc gia/cơ sở được giữ nguyên trong phần nguồn bên dưới.

### SUMMARY

Every newborn has the right to a healthy start in life; however, newborn mortality is still a grave concern in many parts of the world. In the World Health Organization (WHO) Western Pacific Region, the rate of newborn mortality is alarming, with one newborn death every 5 minutes in 2021. Most of these deaths are preventable with simple and cost-effective interventions. The Action Plan for Healthy Newborn Infants in the Western Pacific Region (2014−2020), jointly published by WHO and the United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF), was endorsed by Western Pacific Member States in 2014 to ensure a healthy start for every newborn by implementing Early Essential Newborn Care (EENC). The set targets of the Action Plan have since been extended to 2030. Eight priority countries in the Region (Cambodia, China, the Lao People’s Democratic Republic, Mongolia, Papua New Guinea, the Philippines, Solomon Islands and Viet Nam) with the highest burden of maternal and newborn mortality have been targeted for support in the implementation of the Action Plan. To monitor its implementation, an Independent Review Group (IRG) was established in 2015 to review and validate country and regional progress based on the indicators in the EENC Monitoring and Evaluation (M&E) Framework. The Fourth Biennial Meeting of the Independent Review Group on Validation of Early Essential Newborn Care Progress was attended by six out of seven members of the IRG. The participants evaluated the most recent data from the annual implementation review of EENC as reported by seven Member States: Cambodia, China, the Lao People’s Republic, Mongolia, Papua New Guinea, Solomon Islands and Viet Nam. During the meeting, the progress of implementation of EENC, based on the Monitoring and Evaluation (M&E) Framework, was assessed at both country and regional levels. On the final day, the meeting was expanded to include the respective technical officers from the WHO country offices of the nine EENC implementing countries. These officers, in collaboration with the IRG members, engaged in in-depth discussions to ensure the institutionalization and sustainability of EENC implementation and to establish a country-driven routine monitoring mechanism. Based on the evaluation of the reported country data, the assessment of EENC implementation progress and comprehensive deliberations, the meeting participants developed recommendations on improving data quality and strengthening the institutionalization and sustainability of implementation. Some of the main conclusions from the meeting included the following:

Impact and population coverage of EENC • Data on population coverage and impact measures for newborn health were available and validated for eight countries. New population-based data available since 2019 from five countries (Cambodia, the Lao People’s Democratic Republic, Mongolia, the Philippines and Viet Nam) included indicators on skin-to-skin contact (SSC), drying and early bathing. The proportion of newborns receiving immediate SSC is now greater than 70% in Cambodia, Mongolia and the Philippines, but remains below 50% in the Lao People’s Democratic Republic and Solomon Islands. • Newborn mortality in seven countries reduced by between 12% and 56% in the previous five years. For the Lao People’s Democratic Republic, Mongolia, Papua New Guinea and the Philippines, comparisons with previous survey data show improved trends in breastfeeding within one hour of birth and exclusive breastfeeding in the first month of life. • Overuse of caesarean section remains a problem in many hospitals in the Region. Population- based caesarean section rates increased to 20% or higher in five countries (range: 20% to 40%), with rates higher in urban areas. In three countries, rates remain below 10% (range: 3% to 7%) and are stable.

• Hospital impact measures were reported by seven countries, with most collected by routine information systems. However, as was the case in 2019, only a small fraction of all hospitals routinely report hospital EENC impact data. Hospital health management information system (HMIS) assessments in four countries show numerous data gaps, non-recording of stillbirths, and systems problems preventing effective data collection on birth weight categories, admissions to neonatal intensive care units, and sepsis and asphyxia cases and deaths.

EENC scale-up readiness • Data on scale-up readiness were available and validated for seven countries. Improvements were noted in all benchmarks, except for regular meeting of the EENC technical working group and formation of an EENC stakeholder group. Six countries had incorporated EENC in pre-service training curricula, which is an improvement from four countries having done so in 2019. Quality of integration into pre-service education remains uncertain.

EENC clinical practice • EENC health facility standards were available and validated from seven countries. • An estimated 6446 facilities have implemented EENC − a 7% increase from 2019. EENC has begun in 80% of eligible facilities in four countries and 73% of eligible facilities in another one. • An estimated 42 403 staff have been coached in EENC across the Region − a 22% increase from 2019. EENC hospital teams have been formed in 75% of hospitals, with 34% of hospitals conducting routine quality reviews. • The proportion of women and term babies receiving any SSC (93%) and immediate SSC (73%) did not change from 2019. Improvements were noted from 2019 in the proportion of babies breastfed while in SSC (33−64%), having 90 minutes of uninterrupted SSC (37−47%) and immediate and prolonged SSC (29−38%). • The proportion of stable preterm babies receiving any SSC (69%) was unchanged from 2019, while the percentage of those receiving immediate SSC (49%) declined. Improvements from 2019 were noted in preterm babies receiving a quality breastfeed without separation (13−30%) and uninterrupted SSC for at least 90 minutes (18−24%). Any Kangaroo Mother Care (KMC) was received by 56% of preterm babies reviewed − an increase of 8% from 2019. Continuous KMC (at least 20 of the previous 24 hours) was reported in 40% of cases − an increase from 10% in 2019. • The proportion of babies born by caesarean section receiving key practices showed improvements from 2019 for uninterrupted SSC for at least 90 minutes (18−29%) and completion of a breastfeed before separation (20−55%), with no changes for any SSC (73−77%) and SSC within one minute (43−41%). • Breastfeeding practices for term babies rose from 2019 for breastfeeding at 15−90 minutes after birth (57−70%) and the first breastfeed of at least 15 minutes’ duration (52−59%). Exclusive breastfeeding before discharge declined from 78% to 70%. Non-recommended bottle feeding remained stable (13−11%) during the same period. • Percentages for breastfeeding practices for preterm babies were lower than for term babies but showed small improvements from 2019 in breastfeeding 15−90 minutes after birth (32−37%) and a breastfeed of at least 15 minutes (35−41%). Exclusive breastfeeding before discharge was unchanged at 75%. Non-recommended bottle feeding remained stable (14−12%) during the same period.

• Effective delivery practices improved from 2019 for companion of choice (26−50%), adoption of a non-supine position in the second stage of labour (57−80%) and encouraging food and fluids (66−80%). Correct completion of the partograph declined from 54% in 2019 to 50%. Harmful practices − enema (17−14%) and fundal pressure (18−16%) − showed little change between 2019 and 2023. • Episiotomy was performed in 24% of deliveries across the Region (range: 27% to 24%) with little change from 2019.

EENC monitoring and evaluation − indicators, methods and tools • The representativeness and quality of Annual Implementation Review (AIR) data were highly variable between countries, with some problems noted with small sample sizes and data calculation errors. The recommended AIR process − including immediate facility feedback and national participatory data review for planning − was not conducted in all countries. Aggregate practice data across the Region included only seven of nine implementing countries. • EENC practice indicators have been incorporated into routine health information systems in three countries (Cambodia, the Lao People’s Democratic Republic and Mongolia). Discrepancies were noted between self-reported DHIS2 data and AIR data from the first two countries but not from the last. • Routine hospital impact data remain highly variable between countries, with common gaps in case definitions, accuracy of recording, completeness of reporting, timeliness of reporting and summary and use of data. Definitions of newborn deaths, stillbirths and birth weight categories are not always consistent between and within countries. • A draft guide on strengthening intrapartum care was reviewed. Principal issues to resolve include: considerable overlap with data already included in the EENC AIR, which should not be duplicated; the need to better define the core package of intrapartum care interventions (preferably as a clinical coaching package); and ensuring that proposed tools and methods will collect valid and reliable data.

### 1. INTRODUCTION

1.1 Meeting organization The Fourth Meeting of the Independent Review Group on Validation of Early Essential Newborn Care Progress was held in Manila, Philippines, from 17 to 19 June 2024. Six of the seven members of the Independent Review Group (IRG) from China, Japan, Papua New Guinea, the Philippines, the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland, the United States of America and Viet Nam attended the meeting. A list of participants is available in Annex 1.

1.2 Meeting objectives (1) to evaluate the most recent data from the annual review of EENC implementation as reported by Member States; (2) to assess the progress of implementation of the Monitoring and Evaluation (M&E) Framework and provide guidance on its institutionalization and sustainability; and (3) to develop recommendations on improving data quality in Member States.

### 2. PROCEEDINGS

2.1 Opening session The participants were welcomed by Dr Kubota Shogo, Coordinator of the Maternal Child Health, Quality and Safety (MCQ) unit of the Division of Health Systems and Services (DHS) at the WHO Regional Office for the Western Pacific. He briefed meeting participants on the agenda items for the following three days, as well as the anticipated outcomes of the meeting. Following the introduction of the participants, Dr Maria Asuncion Silvestre was elected as Chair of the meeting. Dr Shogo gave a presentation on progress made towards implementing the Action Plan for Healthy Newborn Infants in the Western Pacific Region (2014−2020), which was endorsed in 2013. This was followed by a presentation by Dr John Murray, MCQ, DHS, on EENC peer-reviewed publications including their scope, lessons and key findings.

2.2 Method The meeting was conducted as small-group reviews of country data, followed by plenary discussions on the institutionalization and sustainability of EENC, and the Intrapartum Care review tool. The programme of activities is available in Annex 2.

2.3 Summary of findings: Review of country data Seven countries submitted data on indicators in the Early Essential Newborn Care Monitoring and Evaluation Framework of the Regional Action Plan in advance of the workshop. The Framework tracks key inputs, outputs, outcomes and impact measures in five data tables: (1) scale-up readiness benchmarks, (2) health facility standards, (3) hospital impact, (4) EENC population coverage and (5) EENC impact indicators. In addition, countries provided supporting documents to validate the status of each indicator. Country data were entered into an offline Excel file organized according to the five tables aligned with the EENC M&E Framework. All supporting documents of each country were systematically compiled to facilitate easy access for the meeting participants during the review and validation process. To conduct the validation, the six IRG members were organized into pairs. Two pairs reviewed data from two countries each, while one pair reviewed data from three countries. IRG members did not

review their own country data. Using the Excel document with all data, IRG pairs reviewed data and supporting documentation for each indicator and indicated whether data were available and validated, and provided any comments including reasons for non-validation if data were not validated. For Tables 2 and 3 of the EENC M&E Framework, the IRG verified primary data and conducted recalculations of indicators. For Tables 4 and 5, which are based on data from population surveys, data provided in summary reports (for example, Demographic and Health Survey reports) and tabulations were considered adequate for validation purposes. Resource persons provided clarifications on country data and context when necessary. Validation of country data was completed on the afternoon of the second day of the meeting.

Plenary discussion on the review of the intrapartum care assessment tool and institutionalization and sustainability of EENC On the third day of the meeting, the IRG, in plenary, commented on the draft intrapartum care tool and discussed the institutionalization and sustainability of EENC with the following focuses: (1) ensuring ongoing country-driven annual implementation review; (2) role of the Regional Office/IRG/biennial meetings in maintaining momentum; and (3) linkage and coordination with other programmes (such as infection prevention and control, quality and safety) and health systems pillars. These discussions focused on technical definitions, people-centred respectful care, standards and programmatic questions related to how the tool will be used in country programmes. The final recommendations of each review and discussion were summarized.

Conclusions and recommendations At the end of the third day, final conclusions and recommendations were developed and reviewed by the group, and next steps were agreed. Reviewers provided final clarifications on any pending issues related to country data in the two weeks following the meeting to develop reports on the validation of EENC data for each country.

2.2.1 Validation of country data and comments on EENC indicators The status of EENC indicators by country are summarized in Tables 1 and 2. Validation findings and comments on indicators are presented as follows:

EENC scale-up readiness benchmarks To track scale-up readiness for EENC, 10 benchmarks are used. All seven countries reported data, with data available for 68.6% of benchmarks overall. Of the completed and partially completed benchmarks, 98.2% were validated (Table 1). However, data were least often validated for the benchmarks “EENC stakeholder group formed” and “EENC technical working group meeting regularly”.

Main findings: § Six out of seven countries have completed a newborn health situation analysis within the last five years and have a mechanism to ensure support of professional associations for EENC. All countries have appointed a full-time EENC focal person within their respective health ministries, conducted an EENC implementation review in the last two years and adapted the EENC clinical pocket guide. Furthermore, all countries had a funded 12-month EENC implementation plan available, though there were challenges in providing documentation to verify the funding status of the plan. Moving forward, attention should be given to sustaining a more functional EENC technical working group and stakeholder network. § Although incorporation of EENC interventions in pre-service curricula remains incomplete in six countries, substantial progress has been made towards this benchmark since 2020. One country successfully integrated EENC into all relevant curricula including medical, midwifery and nursing

programmes. Additionally, five out of seven countries partially incorporated EENC into their respective pre-service curricula. Comments on indicators: § Some countries indicated that their EENC implementation reviews were equivalent to newborn situation analyses. However, it is recommended that EENC reviews should not be considered as newborn situation analyses, as the latter encompasses a broader scope and includes an examination of impact and coverage data. Furthermore, it is important to clearly specify the year in which the situation analysis was conducted. § The data validation review revealed that while all countries had documentation regarding the composition of their EENC technical working group and stakeholder group, they lacked supporting documents to demonstrate the convening of regular meetings for these groups. Consequently, this benchmark was considered an incomplete indicator.

EENC health facility standards A total of 29 indicators are reported for EENC facility standards. Data were available for 91% of indicators across all countries, partially available for only 1% and not available for 8%. Of all indicators, 91% were validated (Table 1). Data validation was not possible for one indicator where the denominator was unavailable. Main findings: § An estimated 6466 facilities with more than 50 deliveries per year are implementing EENC across the nine countries (for 2023, data from the Philippines and Vanuatu were not included) − a 7% increase from 2019. Based on the results of the EENC review as of 2023, four countries (Cambodia, Papua New Guinea, Solomon Islands and Viet Nam) have achieved the regional target of 80% of childbirth facilities implementing EENC (Fig. 1). In addition, it is noted that Viet Nam successfully introduced and scaled up EENC in all health facilities providing childbirth services. § Validated data show that 42 403 staff have been coached on EENC in the seven countries including data from the Philippines and Vanuatu up to 2020 (Table 2). Coaching data for some countries were not complete due to data availability and data quality. For example, some data were not available from different levels of hospitals. Some reported data could not be validated due to concerns about data quality (for example, use of assumptions to estimate numerators or denominators). Data collection methods need to be improved for both staff and facilities to allow coverage to be tracked over time. Denominators should be based on staff currently working in maternity and newborn care areas. Development and use of a standardized electronic system/form for capturing these data was recommended. § As of 2023 an estimated 308 national regional and provincial hospitals (excluding China) have begun implementation of Kangaroo Mother Care (KMC) across the nine countries (cumulative data from the Philippines and Vanuatu). Only two countries (Cambodia and Papua New Guinea) have updates on data regarding the number of facilities scaling up KMC. In eight countries with available data, 47% of staff providing childbirth and newborn care services have been coached in hospitals implementing KMC. § A total of 75% of hospitals had established an EENC hospital team − a 20% increase from 2017. A quality improvement approach was being used in 34% of hospitals − a 15% increase from 2017. § Significant improvements were observed in the delivery practices of priority countries compared to the previous review in 2019. The proportion of women accompanied by a companion of choice during childbirth increased from 26% in 2019 to 50% in 2023. Similarly, the percentage of women adopting a non-supine position during the second stage of labour rose from 50% to 80%, and the practice of encouraging food and fluid intake during labour improved from 66% to 80%. However,

there was no notable change in the correct completion of partographs, which remained at 54%. Furthermore, harmful practices, such as administering enemas (14%) and applying fundal pressure (16%), were reported less frequently compared to 2019, indicating a positive trend in reducing such interventions. A total of 42% of maternity, neonatal intensive care (NCU) and postnatal care rooms had adequate sink handwashing facilities available: clean sinks with running water, soap and disposable towels (980/2322). Of all maternity and newborn care rooms, 76.3% (1772/2322) had alcohol hand gel available in the room on the day of the visit. In most facilities, gaps were noted in postnatal care or recovery rooms, where patients were required to provide their own soap and disposable towels. Most resuscitation areas were clean and dry (520/558 [93%]) and had functional ambu bags and masks (term and preterm) available (549/558 [98%]). Only 1.6% (36/2322) of hospital rooms had baby food company materials visible or available; 5% of hospital rooms (118/2322 [49%]) had hospital policies or orders prohibiting use of infant formula or linkages with milk formula companies displayed in wards or public areas. Health facilities were assessed to determine whether essential medicines and supplies were available on the day of the visit, stored correctly, had stock records available and with no stockouts in the previous 12 months. Facilities fulfilled all these conditions for magnesium sulfate (135/207 [65%]), oxytocin (144/207 [70%]), injectable antibiotics (105/207 [51%]) and routine eye prophylaxis (115/207 [56%]).

Comments on indicators: § Challenges reported by most countries were in reporting data on the number of health facilities that have introduced EENC, and in capturing coaching data. These issues are primarily due to the lack of a proper tracking system and systematic and constructive way of recording results for these indicators. While it was initially manageable to gather these indicators at the early stages of implementation, data accuracy and tracking system reliability decreased significantly over the years as programmes expanded. This issue is prevalent across almost all implementing countries, highlighting the need for improved routine monitoring of these indicators to support accurate data collection and reporting. § Similar issues were observed with country-reported data on the proportion of coached staff. In some countries denominators were even lower than numerators because they included the number of coaching sessions conducted instead of the number of coached staff. A proper digital and tracking system should be introduced for better monitoring and future planning for coaching. § In some countries the definition of non-supine position in childbirth is still unclear. It was observed that some cases applied to both first and second stages of labour. This indicator should be applied to the second stage of labour only. § In some countries, the timing for immediate SSC among babies born by cesarean section was unclear. To standardize this practice, country teams should apply Algorithm 2.2 Essential Newborn Care in caesarean deliveries as outlined in the Early Essential Newborn Care: Clinical practice pocket guide 2nd edition. Country teams are encouraged to review the key updates in this newly published pocket guide.

Hospital impact indicators Nine indicators are reported on hospital impact, and data were reported by six countries. Hospital impact measures were reported by seven countries, with most collected by routine information systems. However, as was the case in 2019, only a small fraction of all hospitals routinely report hospital EENC impact data. Hospital health management information system (HMIS) assessments in four countries show numerous data gaps, non-recording of stillbirths, and systems problems preventing effective data collection on birth weight categories, admissions to NCU, and sepsis and asphyxia cases and deaths. As was the case in 2019, gaps in hospital data continue to be noted. Complete data were available for 35% and partial data for 49% of indicators (Table 1). Frequently, data were not disaggregated by gestational age, birth weight or cause of death. Partial reporting also occurred because not all hospital levels were reported on; 68% of hospital impact indicators with complete or partial data were validated. Clear trends in indicators are not yet discernible within countries, which may be partly due to issues with data quality.

Comments on hospital impact data: The large-scale routine collection of hospital data has not yet been feasible in most countries. However, Mongolia serves as a notable example, having effectively integrated hospital impact data into its routine maternal and child health surveillance system. This system collects data electronically from all facilities on a weekly and monthly basis, demonstrating a commendable approach to comprehensive data management. From the reported hospital impact data, common challenges identified include incomplete recording and reporting, particularly for key data elements such as birth weight and gestational age, as well as the incorrect or inconsistent application of definitions for some indicators including preterm birth and neonatal death causes (such as asphyxia and sepsis). Key factors contributing to gaps and challenges include reliance on paper-based systems, inadequate coordination between data collection processes in maternity and newborn areas, insufficient staff training, a lack of feedback mechanisms for reported data, and absence of robust system for data assurance. The assessment of hospital information systems for childbirth and newborn health in Cambodia (DHIS2), the Lao People’s Democratic Republic (DHIS2), Mongolia (maternal and child health surveillance system) and Viet Nam (HMIS) have provided valuable insights into gaps in data quality, their underlying causes and ways to address them. Moving forward, the focus should be on devising feasible action plans to strengthen the routine health information systems for hospital impact data.

EENC population coverage indicators Thirteen indicators are reported to track population-based coverage. All seven countries reported on the coverage indicators, with data available for 80.2% of indicators; 99% of available data were validated. Indicators most frequently reported by all countries were skilled birth attendance, rates of facility delivery and caesarean section, and mothers receiving postnatal care within two days of birth. Indicators least frequently reported continue to be immediate and postnatal newborn care practices and breastfeeding practices at 0 to 1 month. Coverage data were not updated for Papua New Guinea because a large-sample population-based survey has not been conducted since 2018.

China and Mongolia reported all population-based indicators from routine health information system reports. Therefore, data were not available for several indicators including SSC, breastfeeding initiated within one hour of birth, and exclusive breastfeeding from 0 to 1 month for China. Solomon Islands and Viet Nam reported some population-based indicators from routine health information system reports.

Main findings: Population-based surveys were undertaken after 2020 in five countries − Cambodia, the Lao People’s Democratic Republic, the Philippines, Vanuatu and Viet Nam. Most population-based surveys conducted before 2015 did not include early newborn care indicators. Based on effective advocacy and awareness-raising efforts, programme managers and planners have actively been involved in the questionnaire development process to ensure that national surveys include key newborn care indicators. As a result of these collective efforts, recent population-based surveys now include new indicators on SSC, drying and early bathing practices. For the Lao People’s Democratic Republic, Mongolia, Papua New Guinea and the Philippines, comparisons with previous survey data show improved trends in breastfeeding initiation and exclusive breastfeeding in the first month of life. In countries with population-based data on SSC, rates are well above 50% in Mongolia (79%), the Philippines (71%) and Solomon Islands (50%), and below 50% in the Lao People’s Democratic Republic (14%), Papua New Guinea (43%), Vanuatu and Viet Nam (13%). Overuse of caesarean section remains a challenge in some countries of the Region. In seven out of nine countries with new data since 2019 (Cambodia, China, the Lao People’s Democratic Republic, Mongolia, the Philippines, Vanuatu, and Viet Nam), population-based caesarean section rates have increased by 0.9−11.6%, with rates higher in urban areas. Estimated caesarean section rates remain above the recommended target of 10% of live births in five (Cambodia, China, Mongolia, the Philippines and Viet Nam) of the nine countries.

EENC impact indicators Impact indicators for tracking newborn health impact were reported by all nine countries (Vanuatu and the Philippines were included due to the availability of the recent population-based survey report). Sources of EENC impact data included the United Nations Inter-agency Group for Child Mortality Estimation (IGME) (for neonatal mortality estimates and causes of death), population-based surveys, and routine surveillance data in countries with relatively high system coverage and reporting (China and Mongolia). Since 2019, data from new population-based surveys were available for five countries − Cambodia, the Lao People’s Democratic Republic, the Philippines, Vanuatu and Viet Nam. Modelled estimates were used to estimate preterm birth rates and low birth weight rates in some countries. A total of 92% of impact indicators for newborn health were reported by countries. Still, data were most frequently unavailable for perinatal mortality; 88% of available data were validated (Table 1).

Main findings: Newborn mortality is estimated to have decreased by 11–56% in eight out of nine countries since the adoption of the Action Plan in 2013, based on modelled estimates by IGME. Largest estimated declines are noted in Cambodia and the Lao People’s Democratic Republic (Fig. 4).

### 2.4 Summary of discussions

2.4.1 Institutionalization and sustainability of EENC implementation The participants agreed on the following recommendations: § Ensure that EENC practices (for example, immediate and thorough drying, delayed cord clamping, SSC and early initiation of breastfeeding) are mandatory in the pre-service curricula of medical, nursing and midwifery programmes. § Expand the integration of EENC into pre-service curricula for all health professionals to ensure a skilled workforce at the entry level. § Coordinate with national health authorities to integrate EENC into standardized competency framework for respective health workforce. § Institutionalize ongoing in-service training, mentorship and supportive supervision for health workers to maintain skills and confidence in EENC practices. § Promote collaboration across multidisciplinary delivery team members (obstetricians, midwives, neonatologists, neonatal nurses and others) to apply peer learning and coaching for providing good-quality EENC interventions in the delivery room.

§ Strengthen health information systems to track EENC coverage, outcomes and adherence to practices, enabling data-driven decision-making. It is particularly important to integrate EENC core indicators into routine health information systems. § Strengthen country-driven mechanisms for routine monitoring of EENC implementation and periodic evaluations to identify gaps and best practices. Country capacity for conducting EENC implementation review should be advanced. § Build capacity among health facility staff for accurate data collection, analysis and interpretation of EENC quality metrics. § Explore innovative solutions, such as digital health tools, to enhance EENC training, monitoring and family engagement. § Use continuous quality improvement approach tools, including Module 4 and Plan-Do-Study-Act cycles to address EENC adherence. § Tie adherence to EENC quality standards to facility accreditation and recognition programmes to incentivize compliance.

2.4.2 Facility assessment tools for basic intrapartum care The participants drew the following conclusions: § Intrapartum care is an area of gap, and investing in it could potentially save more lives. Promoting basic routine care is crucial because emergencies usually do not happen suddenly. Simple changes, such as encouraging behaviour changes among health-care workers (for example, advising against women lying on their back during labour), can be implemented. § While comprehensive assessments using the tool could provide insights, having more than 300 questions can be overwhelming. There are clear gaps in areas such as equipment, sterilization and hygiene practices. Supporting implementation efforts may have more impact than focusing solely on assessments. § The EENC initiative was originally designed as a coaching tool, not an evaluation method. The Annual Implementation Review (AIR) is a cross-sectional assessment that has undergone repeated testing and revisions. It is important to clearly define changes that need to be made and how they can be measured in a valid way. § Investing in quality of care is crucial; however, in Papua New Guinea, it is also vital to address the reality that over half of women give birth without receiving any care at all. § In the Philippines, maternal mortality is related more to the quality of care than just coverage; therefore, facility assessment tools may be useful. § Approaches should be tailored to each country’s specific situation.

### 3. CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS

3.1 Conclusions Seven of nine priority countries in the Region (Cambodia, China, the Lao People’s Democratic Republic, Mongolia, Papua New Guinea, Solomon Islands and Viet Nam) have continued widespread scale-up of EENC and all submitted data for review. The Philippines and Vanuatu did not submit data for the review. However, published population-based survey data from the Philippines and Vanuatu were included. Across the Region great progress was noted in improving immediate and uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact for 90 minutes, breastfeeding before separation, breastfeeding 15−90 minutes and other EENC practices, with notable improvements for preterm and low-birth- weight babies and those born by caesarean section.

### 3.2 Recommendations

### 3.2.1 Recommendations for Member States Member States are requested to consider the following:

(1) Update EENC M&E data based on IRG recommendations in preparation for the Member States’ Fourth Biennial Meeting on Accelerating Progress in Early Essential Newborn Care in November 2024. (2) Develop a multidisciplinary approach for incorporating EENC interventions and practice methodologies (clinical coaching-based) into pre-service education curricula for public and private training institutions. This approach should include building the capacity of educators, updating accreditation and regulatory standards, and providing regular curriculum updates. (3) Investigate incorporation of EENC impact and practice indicators into routine data systems where feasible. Continue to strengthen routine reporting through DHIS2 systems to improve the quality of self-reported data. (4) Continue biennial EENC implementation reviews for planning and tracking progress (EENC Module 1) in countries in the early introduction and scale-up phase. For countries with more established EENC programmes, consider implementation reviews every three to four years. Improve the capacity of ministry of health staff and local EENC facilitators to conduct EENC implementation reviews routinely as intended to ensure data quality, immediate local use of data and action planning. (5) Support continued scale-up of EENC and KMC for babies born preterm and by caesarean section through continued clinical coaching and systems upgrades (EENC Module 4); developing and enforcing non-separation guidance and clinical practice protocols; routine collection and use of simple practice data to identify and target practice and systems gaps by hospital-based multidisciplinary teams; incorporating practice standards into accreditation standards and mechanisms; identifying and managing other systems barriers; and using regional South−South exchange visits to high-functioning sites.

3.2.2 Recommendations for WHO WHO is requested to do the following: (1) Send country reports of the validation to national focal points, to clarify data gaps and inconsistencies. (2) Conduct country consultations to determine intrapartum care gaps and challenges and determine next steps for improving intrapartum care practices. (3) Continue to support Member States to institutionalize M&E for EENC in the area of HMIS, strengthening quality of EENC implementation reviews as a part of routine programming and ensuring routine use for improving quality of care at all levels. Expanding the scope of the AIR data collection tools and methods to include new intrapartum care data (not currently included) is not recommended, since doing so risks changing the primary focus from programme quality improvement and capacity-building to a data-gathering exercise. The primary goal of the EENC AIR process – rapid, focused data collection and immediate use by facility and programme staff to identify and address gaps – should be preserved. (4) Consider developing a targeted strategy and clinical coaching approach to support the improvement of intrapartum and postpartum care focusing on key evidence-based practices, using similar methods as proven effective for EENC implementation and scale-up. Ensure this approach is developed using bottom-up methods based on practical realities in country settings.

(5) Continue to conduct EENC data validations followed by Member State meetings every two to four years. (6) Finalize, publish and widely disseminate findings of the EENC validation workshop and Member State meeting as technical reports and peer-reviewed journal articles to document progress and encourage wider adoption of the approach. (7) Give greater attention to developing EENC advocacy and communication materials and messages targeted at decision-makers – to better inform development partners and country staff on EENC progress, materials and methods. (8) Review the role of the IRG in supporting countries in the next five years to increase advocacy and technical support for continued and wider adoption and integration of EENC into routine programming.
