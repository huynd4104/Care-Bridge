# WHO 2024 — Mạng lưới chăm sóc sức khỏe bà mẹ và trẻ sơ sinh: hướng dẫn triển khai


## Nội dung trọng tâm đã Việt hóa


WHO mô tả “mạng lưới chăm sóc” như một cách tổ chức các cơ sở và dịch vụ liên kết với nhau để người mẹ và trẻ sơ sinh nhận được đúng chăm sóc, đúng nơi, đúng thời điểm. Hướng dẫn nhấn mạnh sự phối hợp giữa các cấp chăm sóc, cơ chế chuyển tuyến và phản chuyển tuyến, giao tiếp và chia sẻ thông tin, tiêu chuẩn chất lượng, trách nhiệm quản trị, nhân lực, hậu cần và theo dõi kết quả.


Với CareBridge, tài liệu này đặc biệt hữu ích cho logic phân tuyến: ứng dụng không nên chỉ đưa kiến thức; khi phát hiện nhu cầu vượt khả năng tự chăm sóc hoặc cơ sở hiện tại, hệ thống phải hỗ trợ kết nối tới cấp phù hợp và duy trì tính liên tục thông tin. Nội dung là hướng dẫn triển khai hệ thống, không phải phác đồ xử trí cá nhân cho sản phụ.


## Ranh giới sử dụng cho RAG


- Giữ nguyên năm xuất bản, quốc gia/khu vực, thiết kế nghiên cứu và đối tượng khi truy xuất số liệu.
- Không biến kết quả nghiên cứu, khảo sát hoặc tài liệu chiến lược thành chẩn đoán/phác đồ cho cá nhân.
- Khi có xung đột với hướng dẫn hiện hành, ưu tiên tài liệu hiện hành đã được thẩm định.
- Phụ lục nguyên ngữ bên dưới được giữ lại để hạn chế mất tri thức và hỗ trợ kiểm chứng.

Executive summary
While notable progress has been made to improve access to, and the availability and quality of, 
maternal and newborn health (MNH) services, catalytic approaches are needed to support countries 
in continuing to reduce preventable morbidity and mortality and reach the Sustainable Development 
Goals (SDGs). NoCs are one approach for strengthening primary health care (PHC) and MNH services. 
The approach focuses on cultivating relationships among the different stakeholders and strengthening 
relational elements, such as multidisciplinary teamwork, leadership and communication, which 
differentiates NoCs from other health system strengthening approaches, such as referral and 
private sector engagement. NoCs exist in diverse contexts and geographic regions. Existing studies 
have demonstrated their effectiveness in improving quality of care and clinical outcomes for mothers 
and newborns.
Due to the increasing number of NoC examples, WHO and the NoC Steering Committee 
collaborated to develop this guide on establishing and strengthening NoCs. This document aims to 
guide Ministry of Health officials (at national and subnational levels), implementing partners, 
community-based organizations, health workers, academia, and professional associations through 
the key steps to establish and strengthen a NoC. While the guide is meant to be context agnostic, 
examples throughout the document highlight different approaches for maternal and newborn care. 1
1. Introduction
1.1 Background
Ensuring PHC and maternal and newborn care that is high-quality, comprehensive, and accessible to 
people is essential for a population’s health and well-being. NoCs are a promising approach through 
which to achieve this. 
1.1.1 Primary health care
PHC is a whole-of-society approach that aims to maximize the level and distribution of health and 
well-being. Along with essential public health functions, PHC is at the center of the full range of 
integrated health services and empowers people and communities. PHC delivers 90% of universal 
health care (UHC) services (1).
A model of care represents a set of strategic choices that determine how services are delivered, 
including through processes of care, service design, organization, management and community 
linkages and engagement. An intentional and well-designed model of care identifies the roles and 
responsibilities of different platforms, networks and providers along pathways of care. There is a shift 
towards national and/or subnational PHC-oriented models of care centered on high-quality primary 
care, promoting strong linkages with timely acute care and effective referral and counter-referral 
systems. NoCs are an approach for strengthening models of care and are an operational lever for 
PHC. They also foster stronger connections with tertiary care. 
1.1.2 MNH and NoCs
Globally, investments to reduce maternal and newborn mortality have traditionally focused on 
addressing the first, second and third delays of care (2). As a result of these important efforts, more 
women seek care from skilled health personnel in health facilities. Almost 80% of births globally are 
facility-based (3). However, inefficiencies within the health system continue to impact the quality of 
services and limit progress towards achieving MNH goals. 
Maternal and newborn mortality is an issue of access to care as well as the quality of services 
provided (4). New approaches are needed to create more efficient and effective systems that deliver 
quality, person-centered care at each point, ensuring continuity of care between community and 
hospital settings. 
Interactions and relationships between community, health workers and facilities are needed to ensure 
quality, accountability and effective movement of the women and newborns through the health 
system. Care for women and newborns requires connections at each transition between health 
workers, other health facility staff, health system levels, packages of care, family members and the 
woman and newborn. There is increasing recognition of the need for collaborative management, 
relational linkages and strategic coordination of services along the continuum for all mothers and 
newborns in order to improve quality of care and outcomes (5).
While notable progress has been made in improving maternal and newborn outcomes over the past 
30 years, to achieve the SDGs, additional transformational and catalytical approaches are needed. 
The NoC approach leverages existing health system strengthening strategies, with the incorporation 
of a relational focus, to strengthen quality of care and ultimately improve maternal and newborn 
outcomes (6). NoCs can improve and strengthen respectful maternity care. As country interest in 
adopting NoCs increases, WHO, in collaboration with country experts and technical partners, has 
developed guidance to support the establishment and strengthening of NoCs for MNH. 2 Networks of care for maternal and newborn health: implementation guidance
1.2 Objective of this document
Since 2015, WHO, partners and countries have been working to advance the NoC approach for MNH. 
This includes a scoping study with MNH and PHC case studies (7-12), a landscape review and a NoC 
definition for MNH (6), technical consultations with partners and countries, evaluations of existing 
NoCs and targeted work on NoC relational elements. The guide was developed based on this work.
The objective of this guidance document is to be a tool to guide setting up and strengthening NoCs. 
This document guides Ministry of Health officials at national and subnational levels, implementing 
partners, community-based organizations, health workers, academia and professional associations 
on the key steps to establish and strengthen a NoC. While the guide is meant to be context agnostic, 
examples throughout the document will highlight different approaches, specifically for MNH. 
1.3 Approach to development of this document
This operational guidance on NoC adoption for maternal and newborn health is based on previous 
work on the approach (see section 1.2), the existing literature on NoCs, and country experiences 
establishing and strengthening NoCs. The evaluations highlighted in this guide were identified from 
the scoping study (8) and landscape review on NoCs (6). We drew on discussions at the NoC 
technical meeting in Accra, Ghana in December 2022 and sessions on NoCs at the International 
Maternal Newborn Health Conference in Cape Town, South Africa in May 2023 and the International 
Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics World Congress in Paris, France in October 2023. At the 
NoC technical meeting in Accra, Ghana, participants listed and ranked research priorities for NoCs; 
the highest ranked research priorities are included in section 7, Areas for future research. The NoC 
Steering Committee guided the development and provided valuable input in shaping this document.
1.4 How to use this document
This guide can be used by those interested in establishing and strengthening NoCs in their settings. 
It can serve as a reference document for understanding the NoC approach. Additionally, this guide 
can be used by those interested in undertaking research on NoCs.

2.Defining a NoC for MNH
2.1 What is a NoC for MNH?
“A network of care for maternal and newborn health is a collection of public and/or private health 
facilities and its health workers deliberately interconnected to promote multidisciplinary teamwork 
and collaborative learning in order to provide comprehensive, equitable, respectful, person-centred 
care from home/community to primary through to tertiary levels” (6). 
Figure 1. NoCs for MNH
NoCs are built upon existing structures, actors and linkages within the health system. These connections 
involve communities, health workers, facilities, governments and other stakeholders. A functional 
NoC enables collaborative learning and coordinated continuity of high-quality and respectful care 
from home to tertiary levels of care. NoCs focus on relational aspects to strengthen relationships, 
connections and collaboration within and between cadres, facilities and levels of care to optimize 
linkages and continuity for an efficient and resilient health system. A NoC builds upon the existing 
health system infrastructure. While not designed solely for referrals, a NoC can enhance an existing 
referral system if one is already in place. NoCs facilitate linkages across various care levels, ensuring 
a smooth patient journey from the community setting to more specialized care, if needed. For a NoC 
to function optimally, an enabling environment is necessary. NoCs can reinforce linkages with the 
community and across sectors of care, supporting national and district health systems in establishing 
PHC and achieving UHC (6). The strategies for establishing and strengthening NoCs are context 
driven. This document is focused on NoCs for MNH, the goal of which is to improve maternal and 
newborn survival and well-being. Figure 1 highlights the NoC focus on the relational elements, 
building on the existing structural components and health system. 
The NoC approach complements and supports other health system strengthening and service 
delivery approaches, coexisting harmoniously with them. During the December 2022 NoC technical 
meeting in Ghana, participants highlighted the complementarity between NoCs with various existing

Service delivery redesign is defined as the “intentional reorganization of a health system to improve 
equity, quality and outcomes” (13). Establishing and strengthening NoCs is an implicit and enabling 
part of this process. For example, a NoC can help target marginalized communities, allowing service 
delivery redesign efforts to reach them more effectively. However, there is a key difference in focus. 
Service delivery redesign leans towards strengthening the structural components and processes of 
the health system. This involves deciding which facilities provide specific service packages and with 
what health workforce. On the other hand, NoCs prioritize relational aspects, fostering connections 
and collaboration among community members, health workers and facilities.
Both NoC and quality improvement collaboratives foster collective ownership of patient outcomes. 
Quality improvement collaboratives, defined as groups of professionals from various organizations 
who work together to improve healthcare quality through shared learning and support (14), share this 
characteristic with NoCs. These approaches may utilize similar tools for quality improvement, such as 
Plan-Do-Study-Act cycles, incorporating peer learning opportunities and enhancing data utilization. 
However, quality improvement collaboratives are often time-bound and focused on specific 
improvement areas.
NoCs and EmOC networks both contribute to referral systems, community engagement, partnerships 
across healthcare sectors, inter-facility communication and communication systems. NoCs specifically 
support and leverage linkages between facilities, enhancing referral processes and private sector 
engagement within district health systems. Their focus lies on cultivating partnerships through 
fostering trust, respect and a positive professional culture. EmOC networks, on the other hand, excel 
at optimizing the identification and location of appropriate healthcare facilities for women in need. 
Similar to some quality improvement collaboratives, EmOC networks utilize routine monitoring of 
MNH data to drive quality improvement through Plan-Do-Study-Act activities.
District health systems may integrate NoCs, quality improvement collaboratives, and EmOC networks, 
alongside utilizing service delivery redesign processes. These systems operate hierarchically, 
structured by political administrative units, and receive standardized structures and resources from 
the Ministry of Health, including data systems, financing and human resources. While mentorship 
and supervision may be offered within district health systems, it is often not well institutionalized. 
NoCs enable district health systems to function more optimally. 
2.2 NoC structural and relational elements
NoCs are built on structural elements but have a focus on relational elements (Figure 1). Structural 
elements are core health system elements, including the physical environment, leadership structures, 
commodities, policies, clinical norms and standards/guidance, health workforce planning/deployment, 
information systems, referral system and appropriate financing. The NoC approach prioritizes 
relational aspects over structural elements, which are outside the scope of this guide.
Relational elements are the foundation of a NoC. NoC relational elements include intentional 
partnerships, teamwork based on trust and respect, strengthened communication facilitated by 
innovative modalities (e.g. WhatsApp, telemedicine), leadership strategies, collaboration within and 
across cadres throughout the NoC, coordinated person-centered equitable care across the NoC, 
supportive mentoring, cross-learning and sharing, partnerships and equitable care across the NoC.

2.3 Overview of evidence on NoCs
While the number of studies on NoCs is limited, there are several external evaluations of NoCs for 
MNH that suggest a NoC approach improves maternal and newborn quality of care and outcomes. 
This section provides a summary of those NoCs and the results of those evaluations. Annex 1 provides 
an overview table of the evaluation results. For further details on the studies, all publications on NoCs 
are listed in Annex 2. 
A NoC implemented in Indonesia across six provinces significantly reduced maternal and newborn 
mortality rates. This NoC encompassed over 400 public and private facilities, representing over half 
of the country’s maternal deaths. Following a four-year period of NoC adoption and strengthening, 
hospitals within the network saw a 50% decrease in case fatality rates (CFR) from any maternal 
complication (dropping from an average of 5.4 to 2.6 deaths per 1000 cases). Additionally, very early 
neonatal mortality rates (NMR) decreased by 21% (15).

In Cotopaxi, Ecuador, a provincial-level NoC integrated community health agents to improve access, 
quality, and coordination of essential obstetric and newborn care (19). Representatives from local 
parishes and municipal government were engaged to increase their awareness and adherence to 
laws around the provision of coordinated public sector health services. Services in NoC hospitals 
were reorganized to ensure 24-hour availability of emergency obstetric and newborn care. Providers 
were trained and supervised, while work hours were reorganized to expand services. A weekly radio 
program on health behaviors, danger signs and accessing care was broadcast to increase demand 
for services. Traditional birth attendants and community health workers were trained to identify 
maternal and newborn danger signs and risk factors, as well as to refer to health centers. They were 
formally integrated into the NoC to link the community to services. National referral protocols were 
brought up to date and providers were trained on the updated protocols. Quality improvement teams 
were formed of health center staff, community health workers and traditional birth attendants to 
improve access and quality of emergency obstetric and newborn services. 
This NoC in Ecuador demonstrably improved the quality of care for mothers and newborns (19).
Compared to baseline data from a non-NoC comparison area, women receiving counselling 
increased significantly for newborn care (from 8% to 26%), danger signs (12% to 23%) and family 
planning (6% to 18%). In health centers 90% met clinical standards for first antenatal visits, compared 
to 56% at the start of the NoC, and 88% met clinical standards for newborn facility ambulatory visits, 
compared to 73%. There was also an improvement in clinical quality for outpatient maternal 
postpartum visits at health centers (68% to 98%) and hospitals (0% to 81%). In hospitals, active 
management of the third stage of labor improved from 68% to 97% and 50% (from 13%) of hospitals 
were fully compliant with inpatient immediate emergency neonatal standards. Access to care also 
improved: post-natal visits in the first two days of birth increased by 54% compared to an increase of 
29% in the comparison group. The NMR declined from 5.5/1000 live births to 4.2/1000 live births 
over a period of three years in the NoC while in the comparison group there was a slight increase in 
NMR 4.5 to 4.7/1000 live births (19). 
2.4 Examples of establishing a NoC
NoCs are mainly established through two different approaches. In some cases, they may form 
organically from the bottom up, such as the Perinatal Network of Yaoundé in Cameroon (6) and the 
QRP NoC in Metro Manila, Philippines (11). Other NoCs may be initiated in more of a pre-planned 
structured approach, such as in Indonesia, where local government led the establishment and 
oversight of NoCs (16) or the NoC in northern Nigeria, supported by the CHAI and the Government of 
Norway (10,17). The examples of Indonesia and the Philippines are highlighted below.
2.4.1 QRP – Metro Manila, Philippines
In Quezon City, Metro Manila, Quirino Memorial Medical Centre (QMMC) experienced higher hospital 
maternal mortality due to referral challenges, lack of relationships and communication between 
QMMC and midwifery clinics, high patient volume at QMMC, inadequate number of staff, and lack of 
teamwork. These challenges led the head of the obstetrics/gynecology (Ob/Gyn) department to 
create the QRP NoC in 2013. The goal of the NoC was to reduce mortality and morbidity for women 
and newborns. QRP is a two-way public-private partnership with common goals and a process to 
distribute maternity cases by risk profile and location (including by a harmonized antenatal risk 
scoring tool with socio-economic risk factors). First the NoC identified public and private clinical sites 2. Defining a NoC for MNH 7
to be part of the NoC in a 7 to 10 km radius of QMMC. The Ob/Gyn head visited each potential site to 
assess their compliance with local and national midwifery clinic regulations. Memoranda of 
Understanding (MoUs) were signed between QMMC and the midwifery clinics which set out 
responsibilities, relationships and the maternity services offered. Persistent advocacy by the QRP 
leadership enabled hospital management buy-in. There was no outside capital investment; available 
funds were redirected where possible, and UHC policies and processes were leveraged. The NoC 
became an official part of the QMMC Ob/Gyn department. 
The NoC took a broad set of relational and operational approaches to establish and strengthen itself. 
An operational manual was developed to guide QRP activities and management. Communication 
was standardized among NoC members through dedicated messaging groups. Efforts were made to 
improve documentation in referral and improve transport between the NoC using government 
ambulances. Data was collected specifically on QRP to monitor progress. Roles were formalized to 
support institutionalization of the NoC and obstetrics/gynecology residents at QMMC were tasked 
with managing QRP on a rotating basis. Repainting the wards and cleaning by the NoC leaders 
provided an opportunity to promote a new NoC culture focused on compassionate service, 
pragmatism, quality improvement, dedication to service, high standards, collaboration and teamwork. 
Initiatives, such as suggestion boxes and satisfaction surveys were put in place to get feedback from 
women and their families using the NoC. The NoC holds monthly case discussions on referred 
patients and reviews challenging clinical cases to provide opportunities for learning across the NoC. 
Available data shows promising results. In 2014, the QRP NoC MMR was 35 deaths/100 000 live 
births compared to an MMR of 68.71 for Quezon City the preceding three years and a significant 
reduction from the QMMC average (not including midwifery clinics) MMR of 153.75 between 2010–2013. 
Declines were also seen in QRP in newborn mortality from 44.25 (average per year 2010–2013) to 
32 deaths/1000 live births (2014) (11). 8 Networks of care for maternal and newborn health: implementation guidance
2.4.2 Expanding maternal and neonatal survival, Indonesia
With funding from USAID and support from Jhpiego, the Expanding Maternal and Neonatal Survival 
program supported the adaptation of a NoC in 30 districts across six provinces to address challenges 
in maternal and newborn care (16). Each of the 30 NoCs encompassed the district health office and 
all health centers and hospitals in the district, including private facilities. To formally establish the 
NoC, district health offices convened all public and private sector health facilities/providers and civil 
society (CSO) representatives in the district to: 1) generate a shared vision of a NoC and build 
relationships amongst facilities/providers; 2) map existing public and private MNH resources within 
the district to determine the most efficient referral/consult pathways and optimal continuum of care; 
and 3) clarify/define roles and responsibilities of each health center/clinic/hospital within the NoC to 
provide higher-level care, facilitate referrals, and conduct peer-peer mentoring amongst facilities. 
NoC members prepared and signed written MoUs in each district that outlined the agreed-upon 
pathways, roles and responsibilities within the NoC. These MoUs formally integrated private facilities 
and providers into the district referral system and defined roles for CSOs in advocating for policy 
changes. The MoUs served as a mechanism for NoC members and local government to hold each 
other accountable for fulfilling their roles. In addition, each district implemented an electronic 
communication system, SijariEMAS, to facilitate referrals. The NoC leveraged existing multisectoral, 
district-level working groups meetings to convene network members quarterly to review data from 
health facilities within the NoC, problem solve and develop action plans.

4. Financing a NoC
NoCs are financed through different models. Carmone et al (2020) described financing for a NoC as 
the affordability of services for the user and appropriate budgeting for NoC functioning (8). NoCs 
may be funded by outside donors, such as in Indonesia (16) and Northern Province, Zambia (18).
where activities to establish and strengthen the NoC were largely supported by implementing 
partners and donors. A NoC in Nepal (details in section 3) developed an innovative co-financing 
model between a NGO and local government and communities, which helped to build and renovate 
birthing centers, procure equipment and recruit additional staff (7). Other NoCs, for example, QRP in 
the Philippines (11) and the Perinatal Network of Yaoundé (6) formed and continue to function without 
dedicated outside funding. The QRP NoC has redirected funding to support the NoC and leveraged 
the national health insurance scheme to ensure the cost of care for mothers and newborns is 
covered. 
A study evaluating a NoC implemented in Dar es Salaam, United Republic of Tanzania, between a 
private tertiary hospital and 22 government hospitals and catchment facilities yielded promising 
results. The analysis, covering the period from 2010 to 2016, revealed a significant return on 
investment, with a benefit-cost ratio estimated at 6:1. The study also found that the investment 
breakdown consisted of 43% capital investment and 57% running costs. Interestingly, the proportion 
of capital investment decreased over time, while the proportion of running costs increased (9).

the estimated cost to establish and maintain a functional 
NoC by quantifying the necessary inputs using the 
ingredient method. This NoC is across six subcounties and 
includes 60 public and private facilities. It took nine months 
to introduce the NoC, including three months for start-up 
activities and six months to establish functioning, followed

collectively identifying effective referral pathways,

6. Maintaining and sustaining a NoC
NoCs require a dynamic approach to remain effective. This necessitates adjusting common goals as 
the healthcare landscape and clinical needs evolve. Notably, NoCs can play a crucial role in 
strengthening health system resilience in the face of unexpected challenges like natural disasters or 
pandemics. In scenarios, a well-functioning NoC can be essential for ensuring the continuation of 
MNH services, as demonstrated in Nepal during the 2015 earthquake and the Philippines during the 
COVID-19 pandemic (7,11).
For instance, the QRP NoC effectively utilized the network to decongest the tertiary hospital of low-risk 
deliveries and patients. Low-risk patients at the midwifery clinics received more attentive care and 
were less at risk of COVID-19. The tertiary hospital was then better able to manage higher-risk 
patients within the COVID-19 context. Patients’ needs were better able to be met, infection was 
controlled more easily, and hospitals resources more available (11). The reliance of a NoC in these 
complex operating environments may be the most effective approach to adapt to changing 
priorities and demands. 
A NoC may be able to extend the reach of care to additional populations in urban and rural areas, such 
as in Metro Manila, Philippines, rural Nepal, and Dar es Salaam, United Republic of Tanzania (7, 9,11).
If appropriate, the approach could be scaled up to other geographies or nationally, like the Networks 
of Practice in Ghana (26). While the evolution of a NoC will look different for each network and in 
each geography, sustaining the clinical and quality impact of what the NoC has done is essential. 
The following strategies and activities could be considered to support maintaining and sustaining the 
impact of a NoC (27):
y Planning opportunities for reflection and adoption from the outset.
y Create relationships with entities that help to sustain the NoC, e.g. professional associations.
y Institutionalize relationships, processes, and policies that help to sustain the NoC.
y Succession planning for NoC leadership, considering growth and improvement.
y Train other health system actors to create NoCs.
y Linkages with PHC and UHC.
y Motivating microlevel actors, e.g. hospital management.
y Strong government ownership.
y Managing financial uncertainties, e.g. government funding.
y NoC accountability to the community.
y Using existing systems to monitor NoC functioning, performance, and evolution.
y Leveraging existing quarterly meetings to review NoC indicators.
y Using widespread technology, e.g. WhatsApp.
y Creating learning opportunities for staff at lower-level facilities through spending time in other 
facilities in the NoC, in place of external trainings.16

gap, four key areas have been identified as priorities for further research:
1. Effectiveness and impact of NoCs.
2. Financing NoCs.
3. NoC clients and users.

7.1 Effectiveness and impact
Current research on NoCs suggests positive impacts on mortality, quality of care, and service delivery, 
likely attributable to the structural and relational elements outlined in this guide. However, despite evidence 
of improved clinical outcomes, service delivery, and quality of care, there is a lack of substantial research 
on NoCs’ effectiveness in several key areas. These include understanding how NoCs impact the 
satisfaction of both healthcare providers and patients (users), how they influence the overall functioning 
of the healthcare system, and their impact on efficient and responsive service delivery. Additionally, 
further documentation and evaluation of NoCs’ impact on quality of care and clinical outcomes are 
necessary. This will provide a deeper understanding of how NoCs contribute to these improvements.

7.3 NoC users
The goal of a NoC for maternal and newborn health is to improve maternal and newborn survival and 
well-being (6). While there is evidence that a NoC does enable these improvements, there has been 
minimal research or documentation on the perspectives and experiences of NoCs users. The existing 
documentation provides examples of different approaches to take into account the perspectives and 
experiences of NoCs users. For example, private midwifery clinics within the QRP NoC in Metro 
Manila, Philippines routinely solicit feedback from women and families using their services through 
suggestion boxes or satisfaction surveys. By proactively seeking feedback from the midwifery clinic 
users, the midwives are able to maintain a competitive edge and create the type of services the users 
and their families want (11). The NoC in rural Nepal incorporates social auditing, a process whereby 
users are involved in their health facility planning, execution and monitoring and evaluation of 
activities, to ensure accountability and transparency to the population and to assess demand (7).
NoCs in northern Nigeria (10) and Zambia (18) have strong community aspects, which support taking 
into account population needs and tailoring interventions to those needs and realities. For NoCs to 
ensure patient-centered, quality, respectful care, more research is needed to understand the 
perspectives and experiences of NoC users.
