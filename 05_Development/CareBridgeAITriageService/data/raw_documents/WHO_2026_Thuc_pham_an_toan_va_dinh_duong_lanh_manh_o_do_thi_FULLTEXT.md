# WHO 2026 – Hỗ trợ chế độ ăn lành mạnh và thực phẩm an toàn trong môi trường thực phẩm đô thị


## Chỉ mục tiếng Việt và giới hạn áp dụng


Tài liệu WHO 2026 bàn về môi trường thực phẩm đô thị, khả năng tiếp cận thực phẩm lành mạnh và an toàn, bất bình đẳng và bệnh truyền qua thực phẩm, kết hợp can thiệp hệ thống và chính sách. Đây là hướng dẫn ở cấp môi trường thực phẩm/dân số, không phải phác đồ điều trị hay chứng minh một sản phẩm cụ thể an toàn cho thai phụ.


## Nội dung nguồn tiếng Anh (trích xuất và làm sạch)


Supporting healthy diets and safe foods
in urban food environments
Background
More than half of the world’s population lives in urban
areas,1 and this is expected to increase to nearly 70% by
2050 (1). Deep inequalities are common in urban settings.
Some residents, especially those of lower socioeconomic
status, are exposed to public health challenges, such as
poor sanitation, air pollution, urban violence and poor
road safety (2). Urban food environments2 shape and
are shaped by food systems3 that determine residents’
access to healthy diets and safe food. A lack of access
(both in terms of availability and affordability) to healthy
diets and safe food can increase the risk of malnutrition in
all its forms and diet-related noncommunicable diseases
(NCDs) (3). The increase in urbanization is affecting urban
food safety, as densely populated areas can foster unsafe
food environments that lead to foodborne diseases.
1 There is no commonly agreed definition of the term “urban” across countries. For statistical purposes, the UN adopts the criteria used by each
country or territory. For a discussion of the issues around definition, see Box 2 in The State of Food Security and Nutrition in the World 2023 (3).
2 Food environments here refer to “the physical, economic, political and socio-cultural context in which consumers engage with the food system
to make their decisions about acquiring, preparing and consuming food” (14).
3 A food system gathers all the elements (environment, people, inputs, processes, infrastructures, institutions, etc.) and activities that relate to
the production, processing, distribution, preparation and consumption of food and the output of these activities, including socioeconomic and
environmental outcomes (3).
“Health is created and lived by people within the settings of their everyday life;
where they learn, work, play and love.” - Ottawa Charter for Health Promotion
Though the world is united behind Sustainable
Development Goal (SDG) 2 to end hunger, including to “end
all forms of malnutrition”, progress is insufficient. The
global community is currently off track to meet this goal
or any of its associated targets by 2030. Our food systems
continue to fail to deliver healthy diets and safe food for
all. According to latest estimates, global hunger remains
far above pre-pandemic levels, affecting approximately
673 million people, and 2.6 billion people could not afford
a healthy diet in 2024. An estimated 150.2 million children
under 5 years of age are stunted, 42.8 million suffer from
wasting and nearly 35.5 million live with overweight (4,
5). Undernutrition (stunting and wasting) early in life can
result in poor physical and cognitive development and
increased susceptibility to and risk of dying from common 2
infections. Undernutrition in childhood may also increase
the risk of developing overweight and NCDs later in life.
Nearly one third (30.7%) of women aged 15–49 years are
affected by anaemia (5, 6). Meanwhile in 2022, 2.5 billion
adults globally were overweight of which 890 million lived
with obesity, and numbers continue to rise sharply (7).
Recent analysis reveals differing trends across the rural–
urban continuum. Overall, the prevalence of stunting
and wasting is higher in rural areas, while overweight is
slightly more prevalent in urban areas (3).
In addition, elements such as unsafe water used for the
cleaning and processing of food, poor food-production
processes and food handling, the absence of adequate
food storage infrastructure (8), and inadequate or poorly
enforced regulatory standards contribute to the creation
of a high-risk environment for foodborne diseases (9,10).
These conditions may be especially relevant in situations
of migration, forced displacement, natural disasters and
living conditions of poverty and vulnerability. While new
estimates are set to be published in 2026, latest global
estimates from 2010 suggest that unsafe food may cause
600 million cases of foodborne diseases and 420 000
deaths each year. Almost one third (30%) of foodborne
deaths occur among children under 5 years of age. The
World Health Organization (WHO) estimated that in 2021
57.1 million years of healthy life were lost due to eating
unsafe food (11).
Urbanization and nutrition
Urbanization, in combination with other contextual
factors, such as income changes, demographics and
changing lifestyles, is driving changes throughout our food
systems, including food production, food processing, food
labelling, food storage and distribution, aggressive food
marketing and procurement, and consumer behaviour
and consumption.
First, urbanization is associated with changes in the
lifestyles and employment profiles of both women and
men, and with longer commuting times and working
hours, limiting time for at-home food preparation (3).
Second, urban expansion and smaller living spaces,
driven by efforts to meet rising urban populations have
in several contexts result in limited space and inadequate
infrastructure (including little to no access to energy,
safe water, sanitation waste removal, storage etc.), for
fresh food preparation (both at household and at outlet
level). Third, digitalization has affected urban food
environments, introducing new means to promote and
sell food, notably foods high in unhealthy fats, sugars
and/or salt (see Box 1).
Urban food systems are now commonly characterized
by greater access to food vendors, restaurants and
supermarkets, digital food delivery platforms and
high exposure to food advertising, as well as limited
opportunities to grow nutritious foods. The widespread
availability, affordability and marketing of highly
processed or so-called ultra-processed food, often
high in unhealthy fats, sugars and or salt drives greater
demand for such foods (12). Certain urban areas have
been characterized as food deserts where few or no food
shops or outlets sell fresh foods and healthier options
at affordable prices. Other urban areas, known as food
swamps, have a high density of shops and outlets that
serve predominantly highly processed or so-called ultraprocessed food, often high in unhealthy fats, sugars and/
or sodium. Both raise important issues of equity in access
to healthy diets (3). However, even where affordable fresh
produce is available, other factors related to utilization
(cooking space, energy, water and sanitation) mentioned
above may still limit their consumption. Together these
factors shape today’s urban food environments and their
impact on diets and consequently food security and
nutrition.
The food-processing sector and fast-food segment have
grown quickly, partly as a result of the changes associated
with urbanization and the lack of regulatory policies to
reduce the demand for and offer of highly processed or
so-called ultra-processed food, often high in unhealthy
fats, sugars, and/or sodium.
Urbanization is thus characterized by changes in the
types and quantities of food consumed: diets shift beyond
traditional grains to include dairy, fish, meat, vegetables
and fruits, as well as greater quantities and varieties of
processed foods, convenience foods or foods away from
home (3). Thus, urban populations consume more calories
and greater amounts of highly processed and animal
source foods than rural populations. The reliance on
cheaper, highly processed, less healthy options is further
compounded by the high levels of food insecurity faced by
the urban poor, and lower income households who may
spend a significant proportion of their household income

Box 1. The new challenge of meal delivery apps
Rapid economic growth, urbanization and the rise of online services and apps have brought notable changes to
the way we consume and eat food. In particular, delivery services and takeaway businesses have gained significant
popularity over the past decade. The use of online meal delivery services has increased dramatically globally, across
both high and low-income contexts especially since the lockdowns of the COVID-19 pandemic. The growth of meal
delivery apps particularly in urban settings can in part be attributed to the fact that these platforms rely heavily on
volume, meaning that densely populated areas such as cities are a key market. Other contributing factors include
limited time and space for home meal preparation (14).
Evidence shows that out-of-home foods tend to be less healthy than foods prepared at home, are often high in total
fats, unhealthy fats, sugars and/or salt and low in whole grains, legumes, fruits and vegetables (15–17), and now,
increasingly, people are eating these foods at home. Consumers have become less conscious of the disconnection
between the idealized online presentation of a meal and the food they receive and consume, likely in part due to
the pervasive and often misleading marketing on such platforms (18,19). This disconnection lowers consumers’
attention to potential health and food safety risks because the trust in technology often translates into trust of the
delivered food. The challenge in delivery and takeaway is that food safety aspects must be understood across the
value chain from the kitchen to the delivery service all the way to the consumer’s home. Foods are often prepared
in questionable facilities, in so-called ghost or dark kitchens out of the sight of the consumer, which may pose food
safety risks (20).
In addition to providing convenience, online delivery platforms increase access to food outlets outside the immediate
food environment. This has implications for the broader food environment by increasing the geographical availability
of both nutritious foods and less healthy foods, potentially contributing to an expansion of food swamps. The same
platforms also often promote food outlets serving or selling foods high in unhealthy fats, sugars and/or salt (21).
Consumers, industry, policy-makers and regulators must understand the risks to nutrition, health and food safety
from delivery businesses and takeaway services and recognize their own position and responsibility to bring about
positive change. Concerted action is needed to implement policies and regulations that are adequately adapted to
this new challenge, which straddles digital and physical food environments. Options include, for example, ensuring
provision of clear, quick and easy to understand nutrition information to consumers, elimination of industrially
produced trans-fatty acid, marketing restrictions, and making healthier choices the most convenient, attractive (for
example by using behavioural insights techniques) (22), and affordable options.
The WHO Regional Office for Europe has proposed a set of actions – for governments, academia, the private sector and
investors – such as introducing labelling schemes, introducing and ensuring access to nutrition information online, or
encouraging the introduction of models that promote healthy and sustainable meals – to improve the nutrition and
health outcomes of meal delivery apps and help create healthier food environments in towns and cities (21).
Urbanization and food safety
Socioeconomic and structural changes in the global
food system, driven by rapid urbanization, especially
in developing countries, are reshaping the nature
and scale of food safety concerns, including the tools
designed to manage them. Urban housing often lacks
appropriate cooking facilities because of limited space.
Extreme examples of this can be observed in urban
areas in both low-income countries such as Bangladesh
(23) and high-income countries such as Japan (24).
Furthermore, densely populated urban areas may
also lack appropriate food storage spaces or access
to adequate infrastructure, including fresh water and
sanitary equipment, contributing to unsafe food handling
and preparation practices at household, but also food
production, processing and retail level. Urban lifestyles
also affect food safety with the greater reliance on out-ofhome and processed foods exposing urban populations
to additional food safety risks due to hygiene lapses and
contaminant risks (25–27). The informal economy in urban
settings poses additional challenges with respect to food
safety and nutrition (see Box 2 below).
Rapid changes to our environment, populations and
practices, including those related to the expansion of
urban areas into animal habitats have led to previously
unknown pathogen transmission profiles, making health
emergency preparedness more important than ever. 4
The COVID-19 pandemic, other recent health emergencies
and natural disasters have all underscored the importance
of emergency preparedness and health security. At the
Seventy-fifth World Health Assembly in 2022, WHO Member
States adopted resolution WHA75.7 on Strengthening
health emergency preparedness and response in cities and
urban settings. The resolution outlines the importance of
cities and urban settings to health security and emergency
preparedness, and focuses on the implementation of the
global Framework for strengthening health emergency
preparedness in cities and urban settings (28).
WHO is committed to supporting the creation of healthier
cities. The WHO Healthy Cities movement recognizes
the importance of action at national and local levels to
place health high on the social and political agenda. The
movement brings together multilevel actors to support
actions to address the social, economic, environmental
and commercial determinants of health and create
healthy urban environments that uphold the human
rights of citizens. It encourages health-in-all-policies, and
investing in health promotion, healthy urban planning,
healthy food environments, green policies, physical
activity, effective disease prevention programmes and
supporting community empowerment (see SPOTLIGHT)

This document is designed to showcase policies and
actions that local governments can implement to
protect the health and well-being of their populations
by transforming their food environments to improve
nutrition and food safety and tackle malnutrition in
all its forms.
Despite the focus on urban environments, many of
the policies and actions can create benefits beyond
urban settings and are also appropriate for government
response in rural and semi-rural areas. The document has
a particular focus on providing policy-makers, programme
managers, health professionals and advocates with
information on actions local authorities can take to
improve the nutritional quality and safety of food served

Food systems are a key area for action to realize the
Healthy Cities vision. In 2021, the UN Food Systems
Summit brought together a diverse range of key
stakeholders to mobilize solutions to create healthier,
more sustainable and more equitable food systems (33).
Actions to transform food systems must place health –
of people, animals and the environment – at their core,
shifting the focus from the quantity to the safety and
nutritional quality of food (34).
WHO is championing the implementation of a set of
game-changing food systems actions, which focus on
improving the nutritional quality and safety of food
along the food supply chain and creating healthier food
environments (35). These are long-standing, evidencebased, cost-effective, scalable and endorsed actions
that are promoted and monitored by WHO. Country
progress in these food policies and programmes is
monitored by WHO, using the Global database on the
Implementation of Food and Nutrition Action (GIFNA).
GIFNA mainly focuses on country-level policies, but also
includes some municipal-level policies; for example, the
sugar-sweetened beverages taxes in various cities and
municipalities in the United States of America (36).
The transformation towards healthier and safer urban
food systems works in parallel (see Box 2 for a closer look
at street foods). Key food safety elements include clean
and hot water, hygienic and clean places for preparation
and consumption of food, sanitary facilities for workers
in food outlets, safe food handling and preparation
practices, and places for safe and appropriate food
storage (37).
Local governments are a crucial actor in both transforming
local food systems and realizing the Healthy Cities vision.
There are many actions local governments, town planners,
urban designers, policy-makers and health departments
can take to transform food systems and make safe and
healthy diets more available, accessible and affordable for
everyone. However, which actions they take will largely
depend on their jurisdictional power, which vary across
contexts and sectors. For example, many cities manage
urban waste, but rarely oversee agricultural policy, which
is often governed at regional or national level. Moreover,
authorities within a sector may be divided across levels
of government, where policies are implemented at
city-level, but funded at national level. This limits local
governments’ ability to effectively address challenges.
Consequently, urban governance uniquely requires
managing both horizontal collaboration (across sectors)
and vertical collaboration (across government levels) (14).
Transforming urban food systems to deliver better health
Box 2. Healthy and safe street foods
Street vending of ready-to-eat foods is common in public urban spaces. Street food is important in socioeconomic
terms, as it allows consumers of low and middle income to access food daily (38) and can be a key source of livelihood
for vendors.
However, street foods do not always contribute to healthy diet and they can pose food safety concerns (3,39). For
example, an investigation of street food vendors in South Africa revealed the presence of microorganisms such as
Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Salmonella and yeasts, indicative of unsafe food-handling practices (40).
Attention is increasingly being brought to the healthfulness of street foods. Several studies under the WHO Regional
Office for Europe FEEDcities project have documented the coexistence of traditional homemade dishes and highly
processed industrialized food products at street food vending sites in urban settings across countries in Eastern
Europe and Central Asia (41–43). The foods available have been associated with higher than recommended levels of
sodium and unhealthy fats (trans-fatty acids) and lower than recommended levels of potassium and availability of
fresh fruit and vegetables. Soft drinks (high in sugars) were also widely available across countries examined.
Recognizing the potential to improve the nutritional quality of street foods and other foods eaten outside the home,
the WHO Regional Office for South-East Asia produced a framework for healthier out-of-home foods in 2022, with a
focus on street foods, small-scale eateries and market stalls (44). Given the many infrastructure and regulatory gaps
in the street food supply chain, interventions appropriate to the specific situation are needed at national and local
government levels to ensure the safety and nutritional quality of street foods (3).6

Box 3. Improving urban food safety in traditional food markets
Many urban areas in low-income countries suffer from the highest per capita rate of foodborne illnesses in the world.
From a public health perspective, the riskiest foods are animal source foods, fresh fruits and fresh vegetables. These
food products are sold in various environments, including formal and informal markets, and can often be the source
of a food safety risk. Particularly traditional markets play a central role in ensuring access to safe, nutritious and
culturally appropriate food, promoting health and preventing disease. However, limited availability and access to
safe food that supports a healthy diet in many markets often hinders the adoption of nutritionally adequate diets.
Traditional food markets have also been associated with foodborne and zoonotic (animal origin) disease outbreaks
(47), including the most recent COVID-19 pandemic.
Improvements in urban food safety will require targeted policies addressing traditional food markets including
infrastructure such as adequate water supply and drainage systems, waste management, designated areas for
cooked and raw foods, and proper lighting and ventilation. Policies should also cover wildlife trade, slaughtering of
animals in markets, product traceability and raising awareness of the shared responsibility for food safety among
regulators and food chain actors –including national and local authorities, food inspectors, market managers, food
handlers, and vendors – while considering the unique characteristics of each urban environment (47,48).

Urban food systems face unique challenges that require
specific policy approaches. High population density and
their cities’ roles as hubs of trade, commerce and the
movement of people, goods and animals, make urban
areas especially vulnerable to risks at the human–animal–
environment interface. With up to 70% of all food grown
globally destined for urban areas (52), cities are ideal
environments to foster systems transformation in support
of healthier diets. These policies, for example, must
address the high density and accessibility of food vendors,
restaurants and supermarkets selling convenience or socalled fast foods and the accompanying high exposure
to food marketing in urban settings. Policies must aim
to increase urban access to healthy and safe fresh foods.
This may be easier to achieve in contexts where the
urban-rural continuum is more fluid, and where larger
and smaller cities and rural areas are better connected.
A city’s size, proximity and connectivity to surrounding
rural areas influences the types and costs of available
foods, as well as the livelihoods of rural producers.
Currently, only a minority of urban residents (around
30%) can meet their food needs locally (within 100 km),
while around 80% of food demand is met regionally
(within 500 km) (3). Shorter food supply chains could
improve access to fresh produce and other nutrientdense perishables for urban populations while reducing
food waste (see Box 4). In contrast, reliance on longer
or international supply chains is often associated with
increased consumption of highly processed or so-called
ultra-processed foods, which are often high in unhealthy
fats, sugars, and/or sodium. Given the growing awareness
of the unsustainable and inequitable aspects of current
urban food systems, the concept of local or “territorial”
food systems has gained momentum (3).

Box 4. Creating edible cities
Some cities have introduced initiatives to increase access to fresh, healthy foods. To support these efforts, local
governments should collaborate with other subnational entities (provincial, regional, etc.) and national bodies to
establish and promote policies that encourage healthy, safe food options in urban settings, regardless of food origin
(domestic or international) (14).
The city of Andernach in Germany is one of a growing number of “edible cities” (54). The city motivates its residents
to help plant and grow fruits and vegetables that anyone can harvest. Andernach’s edible city gardens are located
along old city walls, in public green spaces. Vegetables, nuts, legumes, fruits, including berries, and herbs are grown,
and the public is allowed to pick most of the produce free of charge. The edible city concept is intended to support
local food security and resilient and sustainable food production. In addition, planting edible plants in urban
environments may bring environmental benefits — such as improved biodiversity – and social benefits, including
by raising awareness of healthy diets and promoting fruit and vegetable intake (54).
There are also advantages to policies developed and
implemented at the local level by city administrations.
Within local governments, decision-makers are closer to
their communities and better empowered to enact policy
and programmes to tackle problems in the local context
and produce positive outcomes for people. Through
local governments, communities have an opportunity to
identify their own problems, share individual experiences
and communicate these to those who can act. When
communities are enabled to assume greater responsibility
for identifying health issues and given greater control
over advocating for, managing and addressing them —
such as by participating in open consultations on policies
— they are better empowered and motivated, which in
turn fosters more sustainable outcomes (55). Effective
and innovative action at the city level can also inspire
implementation of similar measures at the national level.

using nudges (techniques based on behavioural
insights) to encourage healthy choices.
The rationale and evidence base for many of these policy
areas, along with examples of real-world experience
and practical pointers for implementation, are set out in
previous WHO briefs (35,56–59). Two special focus areas
– healthy public food procurement and service policies
and the application of land-use, planning and zoning
regulations – are further explored in this document.
Examples of some other areas for policy action for healthy
and safe urban food environments are also provided.
Box 5. Creating breastfeeding-friendly cities and towns
Developing breastfeeding-friendly places and promoting a safe breastfeeding environment is vital in cities across
the world. Breastfeeding, privately and publicly, must become a social norm, enabling women to breastfeed in
comfort anytime, anywhere. Research suggests that there is a feedback loop, whereby creation of high-quality
environments that support breastfeeding can change attitudes, as women are reassured to see other mothers
breastfeeding. Measures to design and create breastfeeding-friendly towns and cities are not limited to creating
dedicated breastfeeding spaces; they also include facilitating breastfeeding in workplaces, parks, shopping centres
and community buildings. This means spaces that are accessible, enable breastfeeding mothers to feel dignified,
safe and physically comfortable, and are compatible with other needs and responsibilities, such as looking after
other children (60).

Local governments have a unique opportunity and
responsibility to implement healthy and safe public food
procurement and service policies to ensure that food
served or sold in public settings contributes to healthy
and safe diets. It is equally important that public funds are
spent on foods that contribute to healthy and safe diets.
The reach and substantial purchasing power of local
government institutions — potentially including schools,
health care facilities, sports venues, childcare facilities
and residential care homes — creates potential for local
food procurement and service policies to have a profound
impact beyond city limits, across food supply chains (61).
Implementing healthy and safe public procurement and
service policies can improve public health and nutritional
status by increasing the availability and consumption
of healthy and safe food options in public settings and
decreasing the availability and consumption of less
healthy options (62–66).
Equally, the focus for local governments should be on the
provision of tools and activities that foster healthy and
safe food environments. It is a basic human right to have
access to safe nutritious and healthy food (67).
In addition, such policies can contribute to sustainability
(68), increase productivity in workplaces (69), improve
educational outcomes in schools (70,71) and increase
equity. Potential savings on health care expenditure and
avoidance of lost productivity may also create benefits
for economies (72–74). Public procurement policies that
include criteria such as preference for locally produced,
seasonal and sustainably sourced products, avoiding
overpackaging, recycling packaging and minimizing
food waste, can boost agriculture and food supply
chains, increase the availability and affordability of fresh,
nutritious foods and contribute to more sustainable
food systems. Implementation of such policies allows
local governments to demonstrate their commitment
to achieving healthy populations and can help improve
linkages between rural and urban areas.
Local governments can implement healthy and safe public
food procurement and service policies by, for example:
developing healthy food procurement policies that
establish nutrition criteria to increase the availability
of food that promotes healthy diets and/or limit or
prohibit the availability of food that contributes to
unhealthy diets (such as foods high in salt, sugars and
fats, particularly trans-fatty acids or saturated fats) in
public settings (Box 6);
setting and adopting nutrition standards in public
settings (e.g. schools, hospitals, childcare facilities,
residential care facilities and government offices) to
foster healthy dietary patterns and behaviour and to
prohibit sales of sugar-sweetened beverages or foods
high in fats, sugars and salt in government buildings
and city-owned properties (including in vending
machines);
creating healthier and safer food service
establishment environments (e.g. mandating
calorie and nutrient labelling on menus or shelves,
introducing an easily accessible and comprehensible
food safety labelling system and removing saltshakers and high-salt condiments) and using nudges
to steer customers towards healthier options;
integrating sustainability criteria into food
procurement policies, such as prioritizing organic, fairtrade, and environmentally friendly food options;
introducing and implementing food safety
monitoring mechanisms to ensure that food service
establishments comply with food safety regulations;
and
providing training on healthy, sustainable and safe
food procurement and production to empower food
service providers.

Box 6. Core principles of healthy and safe diets
Nutrition criteria for public procurement that supports healthy diets will need to be tailored to the specific local
context and population. As a minimum, however, the criteria should include the following core principles. To be
healthy, diets need to be (75):
Adequate – meaning that they provide sufficient essential nutrients to prevent deficiencies and promote health
while preventing excess intakes.
Balanced – in energy intake, and energy sources, promoting healthy growth, weight and preventing disease.
Moderate – in the consumption of foods that are associated with negative health outcomes including unhealthy
fats, sodium, free sugars, and highly processed or so-called ultra-processed foods.
Diverse – meaning that they should include a wide variety of nutritious foods to ensure nutrient adequacy.
In addition, diets can only be healthy if foods are safe to eat. The proper handling of foods is essential to prevent
and limit the spread of foodborne diseases. Core principles for safer food can be implemented to reduce the risks
that derive from unsafe food. These include (37):
provision of clean and hot water to wash hands before handling food and regularly during food preparation; and
provision of training for food handlers that promotes practices such as:
1. washing and sanitizing all surfaces and equipment used for food preparation;
2. separating raw meat, poultry and seafood from other foods;
3. cooking food thoroughly, especially meat, poultry, eggs and seafood;
4. storing raw and cooked food at safe temperatures; and
5. using safe water and raw materials.
Implementation of healthy public food
procurement and service measures in
urban settings
Action to improve the nutritional quality of food served or
sold in public institutions has been widely recommended
(76,77). Such action is one of WHO’s NCD “best buys” (78)
and SDG 12 includes a target on promoting sustainable
public procurement practices (79). Healthy public food
procurement and service policies, however, are not being
used as widely as they could be at the national level —
and where they are being applied, it is usually limited to
schools.
Some city, municipal or state authorities are, however,
taking action to fill these gaps, to help realize the
untapped potential of healthy public food procurement
and service policies to support healthy diets (Box 7).
Examples include:
prohibition on distribution or sales of high-calorie,
highly processed or so-called ultra-processed foods
to children and young people under 18 years of age
across Oaxaca state, Mexico, and prohibition on sales
or distribution (including in vending machines) of
sugar-sweetened beverages and calorie-dense foods
in schools (80);
a ban on sales of sugar-sweetened beverages in
municipal buildings in Cape Town, South Africa, in
support of a new national tax on sugar-sweetened
beverages (81);
revised nutrition and hygiene guidelines introduced
for city-owned institutions, including schools and
hospitals, in Ouagadougou, Burkina Faso (82,83);
two public food procurement ordinances: one
requiring all city-procured food to meet nutrition
standards with local sourcing, and another banning
junk food and sugary drink sales and promotions to
students within 100 meters of schools, in Quezon
City, Philippines (84,85); and
since 2002 the city of Copenhagen, Denmark,
has pursued a strategy to ensure that 90% of the
ingredients used in the city’s public food system are
organic (45).12
Box 7. Promoting healthy diets in Montevideo, Uruguay
In Uruguay, the city of Montevideo has been working since 2017 on improving availability of fresh, nutritious foods
and “making the healthiest option the easiest option” for its nearly 1.7 million residents (86). In 2018, it issued a
decree requiring at least 10% of the items on restaurant menus to contain no added salt. This complements earlier
measures to prohibit salt-shakers, ketchup and mayonnaise on restaurant tables.
In 2022, the city authorities introduced a decree establishing new nutrition standards for the food and beverages
sold and served in the city’s 48 public institutions and hospitals (87). The decree restricts the sale of unhealthy
food options which are high in fats, sugars and/or salt, and bans the marketing of highly processed or so-called
ultra-processed foods. The authorities complement this decree with tax incentives – institutions that comply with
the standards qualify for tax relief. Workplace food service providers can also achieve “Healthy Canteen” status
by making healthier options available, such as ensuring their menu is primarily made up of natural or minimally
processed foods, providing free drinking water and not advertising unhealthy food options high in fats, sugars and/
or salt (88).
Box 8. Ensuring sports environments are championing healthy diets
Sport and physical activity are key elements of healthy lifestyles, so sports venues ought to be an ideal place to model
and promote healthy diets. Often such sports facilities (including stadia) are integral to local communities, hosting
events and activities throughout the year. In practice, in many venues the foods sold or served do not contribute to
healthy diets, and people, particularly children, are repeatedly exposed to marketing of brands promoting unhealthy
foods high in fats, sugars and/or salt through sponsorship and advertising. For televised sports events, the reach of
such marketing is even wider, potentially reaching children the world over (90,91).
Action is needed to ensure that sports stadia, other spectator sports venues and the places and facilities where
people practise sport or physical activity are healthy food environments. This requires setting nutrition criteria for
food sold, served or marketed at sports events (including by concessions and in vending machines) or in sports
facilities. City and other local government authorities play an important role in funding facilities and regulating
their health and safety. Other important stakeholders include national governments, sports organizations, athletes,
private-sector companies, advocacy groups, and academia.
In British Columbia, Canada, communities received training, resources and technical support to provide healthy
food environments in publicly funded recreation and sports facilities. After the intervention, the proportion of
unhealthy food options high in fats, sugars and/or salt in vending machines declined, the proportion of healthier
food options increased and, compared with communities that had not received the capacity-building support, more
of these communities had a healthy food policy in place, and the communities themselves reported higher capacity
to provide healthy food environments (92). In Melbourne, Australia, “Green Light Eat Right” is a “traffic light”
labelling scheme for food and beverages sold at Melbourne’s leisure centres and events and in vending machines,
with the aim of helping people make healthier food choices and improving food literacy (93).
Concerted action is needed at local, national and international levels to leverage the power of sports to promote
health and champion healthy diets (94).
The WHO Action framework for developing and
implementing public food procurement and service policies
for a healthy diet (61) provides further examples of policy
implementation. It also provides an overview of how to
develop (or strengthen), implement, assess compliance
with, and evaluate the effectiveness of a healthy public food
procurement and service policy. Applying these approaches
in other settings, such as in sports settings is in line
with the WHO settings-based approach, highlighted
by WHO in the 1986 Ottawa Charter (89). The settingsbased approach emphasizes the development of
environments that support population-wide, positive
health-related behavioural changes (Box 8).13
Box 9. Advertising ban on London transport network shows results
As part of measures to help tackle child obesity, in 2018 the Mayor of London – where 37% of 10–11-year-olds are
affected by overweight or obesity (97) – banned advertising for foods and non-alcoholic beverages that are high
in fats, salt and/or sugar on the city’s public transport network, on which 30 million journeys are made every day
(98). Food and drink brands, restaurants, takeaways and delivery services were only able to place advertisements
that promote their healthier products, rather than promoting their brands. A consultation with city residents
found a very high level of public support – 82% of respondents supported the measure. An evaluation conducted
after 44 weeks compared weekly take-home food and drink purchases in London and in another city in the
north of England, where no advertising restrictions were in place. The study found relative reductionsᵃ in energy
(about 4000 kJ lower), sugar (80 g) and fat (58 g) purchased from foods high in fats, sugars and/or salt in London
compared to the other city (99). Complementary measures from the Greater London Authority have included
a “Takeaways toolkit” that advises local authorities to use planning policies to restrict fast-food takeaways in
areas of over concentration of such outlets or where the potential effects on vulnerable groups such as children
and young people are a concern (100).
Special focus area 2: Utilize land-use, planning and zoning regulations
to create healthy diets and safe food in urban environments
Planning processes and the other regulatory powers of
local governments can be applied to positively influence
access to healthy and safe food options in urban food
environments. City and town planners and urban
designers have long had a role in shaping healthy liveable
environments, including by creating opportunities for
active travel and limiting land uses that may create
environments harmful to health, such as alcohol and
tobacco retailing, gambling venues and sources of noise.
The UN Decade of Action work programme calls for
creation of safe and supportive environments for nutrition
at all ages (95). FAO’s Urban Food Agenda recognizes the
role that effective land-use planning, zoning regulations
and legal frameworks can play in improving access to
fresh, nutritious foods in towns and cities (52). The WHO
Commission on Ending Childhood Obesity recommended
action to increase access to healthy food options in
disadvantaged areas (77).
Access to affordable healthy diets can be undermined in
the food deserts and food swamps that are often found in
low- and middle-income countries and in more deprived –
usually urban – areas in wealthier countries.
Planning tools, including zoning bylaws or regulations,
can influence the retail food environment and thus
introduce barriers to the sale and marketing of foods and
beverages that contribute to an unhealthy diet and/or can
be facilitators to the sale of healthier and nutritious food
options. A growing number of city and town authorities
around the world use restrictions and disincentives in
different ways and, in doing so, help uphold the right to
food.
An analysis by the Institute for Physical Activity and
Nutrition (IPAN) at Deakin University, Australia,
commissioned by the Victorian Health Promotion
Foundation (VicHealth), Melbourne, Australia, examined
the application of land-use planning tools to improve
food access and availability (96). This analysis identified a
variety of ways in which city or town authorities can utilize
land-use planning instruments and other legal or financial
tools in support of healthy diets and safe food. These are
categorized into two main types.
1. Creating restrictions for food retailers and outlets
that predominantly sell, serve or market foods and
beverages that contribute to an unhealthy diet (see
example in Box 9):
Rezoning land or placing additional conditions on the
permitted land use at a particular location.
Exclusion zoning to restrict (or control the density of)
hot food or fast-food takeaways.
Placing an outright ban on new food outlets in specific
areas.

Prohibiting location of fast-food restaurants in the

Introducing a healthy outlet recognition scheme based
on set minimum nutrition (and food safety) criteria and
promoting complying outlets via official government
channels.
Box 10. Groundbreaking law to improve access to healthier food for children in Lima, Peru
In 2021, the Metropolitan Municipality of Lima, Peru, introduced an ordinance to create healthier food environments
for children (101). In support of a national law promoting children’s access to healthy diets which came into force
in 2013, the city’s new law prevents areas around schools from becoming food swamps by prohibiting marketing
of foods high in fats, sugars and/or salt within 200 m of schools. The measure also sets out rules to create healthier
food environments within schools, through standards for food served in school and by requiring provision of fresh
drinking water. In addition, restaurants in the city are incentivized to implement practices to reduce salt and sugar
intakes – the Lima Come Sano (Lima Eats Healthy) programme promotes restaurants in which healthy options make
up at least 20% of the items on their menu, calorie-counts are displayed and salt-shakers and condiments are only
provide on request.
Implementation of land-use planning tools
to support healthy diets
Global data on the application of land-use planning tools
at the city level to improve access to healthy diets are
lacking. Given the complex nature of food systems, it is
difficult to measure the impact of specific changes to food
environments. There is a lack of studies that measure
outcomes before and after a change in food environment
and that include a comparison group to robustly evaluate
the impact of such changes (see Box 10 for an example of a
study that does include a comparison group).
There are, however, examples of urban authorities using
land-use planning tools to take innovative action and
setting a precedent to create healthier food environments
in their towns and cities. Interesting case studies identified
by the IPAN/VicHealth analysis include:
Zoning measures implemented by local authorities
in England, United Kingdom of Great Britain and
Northern Ireland, to limit the establishment of hot
food takeaways near schools, youth facilities and/or
recreation parks (102). This was initially facilitated by
the definition of “hot food takeaway” as a category of
business used in national planning rules. Amendments
introduced in September 2020 made hot food
takeaway a Sui Generis use, giving local authorities
greater control to use planning application processes
to ensure healthier food environments (103).
Creation of a “No Fry Zone” in Wicklow, Ireland, to
exclude the construction or operation of new fastfood/takeaway retailers within 400 m of schools or 15
playgrounds. This was facilitated by advocacy support
from the No Fry Zone 4 Kids Community Group (104).
Similar measures have been implemented at the
national level in the Republic of Korea (105) and
Malaysia (106).
Moratorium on permits for new fast-food restaurants
in South Los Angeles, United States of America. After a
temporary ban, between 2008 and 2010, on permits for
new fast-food restaurants in certain areas of South Los
Angeles, the City Council added a footnote to the City’s
General Plan with a moratorium on issuing permits for
new stand-alone fast-food restaurants in these areas.
A 2017 assessment concluded that the “moratorium
did slow the degradation of [South Los Angeles’] food
resource environment” but noted that, because the
measure did not include all fast-food restaurants and
certain geographical areas were exempt, “the industry
has found ways to continue developing and existing”
(107).
Restrictions on food advertising in Mandurah, Australia.
Given high local prevalence of overweight and obesity
and a finding that one third of advertisements within
500 m of schools in Perth, Western Australia, featured
unhealthy products (of which over half were fast food)
(108), the Mandurah City Council adopted a policy to
restrict advertising that “depicts images of unhealthy
food” on illuminated street signs and on bench seats
(109).
Financial and zoning incentives to encourage sales
of affordable healthy food in New York City, United
States of America. The Food Retail Expansion to
Support Health (FRESH) programme is intended to
bring healthy and affordable food options to lowincome and underserved areas of New York City. The
programme uses both financial and zoning incentives
to encourage new and existing grocery stores to sell
fruits and vegetables. The financial incentives include,
for example, reduced land taxes and building taxes,
and sales tax exemption. The zoning incentives allow
stores to have additional floor area, reduced parking
requirements and, in some areas, larger stores. Since
2009, 28 FRESH projects have been approved, and 22
stores have been built and opened in neighbourhoods
with high poverty, unemployment and diet-related
disease rates (110).
Lessons learned from country experience
Several practical recommendations can be drawn from the
case studies above. To maximize the impact of land-use
planning tools for creation of healthier food environments:
ensure the local planning authority has a food retail
classification system and data visualization tool, which
will enable planners to understand the distribution of
existing retailers and identify priority areas for action
on healthy diets and food safety;4
adopt combined approaches that discourage retailers
selling predominantly unhealthy and unsafe options
and encourage retailers selling predominantly healthy
and safe options;
focus on reducing inequalities and providing
opportunities for all – ensuring that all communities
have access to healthy, safe and affordable food
options is critical;
use a health-in-all-policies approach, with
collaboration and input needed from those involved
in health, transport, education and planning, among
others;
better understand the barriers to adoption and feasible
steps to overcome these barriers, by creating and
making available documentation about relevant landuse initiatives, implementation processes, barriers and
enablers; and
ensure stronger evaluation of land-use initiatives,
including process evaluation (96).
Other useful resources have emphasized the importance
of local community participation and collaboration
in developing interventions (111). It is important that
interventions are tailored to the specific context and that
policy-makers can use the experience from case studies
to demonstrate that these tools are already being used to
create healthier food environments. Key enablers of urban
food policies include data collection both before policy
introduction and for monitoring purposes, ensuring that
necessary policy powers and responsibilities exist at local
level and that national policies are supportive, ensuring
participatory processes for policy development, and that
sufficient funding and political commitment exist. These
are described in greater detail elsewhere (112).

Monitoring implementation and evaluating progress
It is important to translate policy into action and then to
monitor implementation and evaluate its impact. Potential
indicators for evaluating actions to create healthier and
safer urban food environments — whether for local or
national action – include:
number or proportion of public settings and their
surroundings covered by nutrition and food safety
standards for foods and beverages being served or
sold;
number or proportion of restaurants and commercial
food outlets covered by programmes to improve the
nutritional value of foods and beverages being served
or sold;
proportion of public settings that are compliant with
nutrition and food safety standards for foods and
beverages;
proportion of restaurants and commercial food outlets
that are compliant with nutrition and food safety
standards for foods and beverages;
number of enforcement actions taken against
violators of policies for healthy and safe urban food
environments;
pervasiveness of marketing of foods and beverages that
contribute to an unhealthy diet (outdoor advertising/
in publicly funded settings/on public transport) since
policy was adopted;
number or density of outlets selling predominantly
highly processed or so-called ultra-processed foods
and beverages that contribute to an unhealthy diet (or
other proxy indicators such as hot food or fast-food
takeaways) in designated areas of concern since policy
was adopted;
number of planning applications approved for outlets
selling predominantly unhealthy highly processed
or so-called ultra-processed foods, often high in
unhealthy fats, sugars and/or sodium in designated
areas since policy was adopted;
number or density of outlets selling fresh and safe
fruits and vegetables and other nutritious food options
in designated areas since the policy was adopted;
number of planning applications approved for outlets
selling fresh fruits and vegetables and other nutritious
food options in designated areas since policy was
adopted (113);
proportion of food served or sold in local public
settings that is locally sourced (within X km/miles);
percentage change in sales of healthier food options/
menu items in a given setting; and
percentage change in population knowledge of, and
attitude towards, healthy diets.

Annex: Approach to development
This document was drafted in response to multiple partner requests for measures to improve diets specifically in urban
environments, with the objective of adapting established WHO nutrition policy recommendations for urban settings.
The need for a more health-focused approach to urban food systems was further underscored by the 2023 State of Food
Security and Nutrition in the World report, which examined how urbanization is shaping today’s food systems. Special
Focus Area 2 on land-use planning and zoning regulations was conceptualized in collaboration with the Victorian Health
Promotion Foundation (VicHealth), building upon their publication “Land use planning as a tool for changing the food
environment” (1).
The methodology for developing this document involved an initial targeted review of existing WHO nutrition policy guidelines
and reports. A subsequent literature search identified additional references on urban health policies and city-level examples
from credible publicly available sources including peer-reviewed publications (PubMed, Google Scholar) and reports from
other non-governmental and United Nations organizations. Key references are cited in Selected Further Reading below and

This document was drafted by the WHO Department of Nutrition and Food Safety in collaboration with other relevant WHO
technical and regional teams. The draft was subsequently shared with external experts in nutrition, urban health and urban
planning (see Acknowledgements), who provided written feedback on clarity, technical content and practical utility. Content
decisions were made by the core development team, guided by alignment with WHO guidance, practical applicability for
urban settings and relevance of received comments. Country and city examples were purposively selected from identified
literature and inputs from WHO regional offices based on their relevance to urban food environments, status (i.e., in effect
and not repealed), geographic representation across WHO regions, and detailed descriptions of relevant policies.
