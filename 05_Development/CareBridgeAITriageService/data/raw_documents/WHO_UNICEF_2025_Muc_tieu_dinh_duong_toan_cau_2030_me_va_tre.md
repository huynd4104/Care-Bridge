# WHO–UNICEF 2025 – Các mục tiêu dinh dưỡng toàn cầu 2030 cho mẹ và trẻ nhỏ


## Chỉ mục nội dung bằng tiếng Việt


### Đây là khuyến nghị điều trị hay tài liệu mục tiêu sức khỏe cộng đồng?


Đây là bản tóm lược chuyên đề của WHO và UNICEF **công bố năm 2025**, giới thiệu việc kéo dài các mục tiêu dinh dưỡng toàn cầu từ 2025 đến 2030, các chỉ tiêu liên quan mẹ và trẻ nhỏ và biện pháp triển khai. Các tỷ lệ hiện mắc, tiến độ và dự báo trong tài liệu đều gắn với năm số liệu gốc, **không phải số liệu thời gian thực**. Các mục tiêu quần thể không phải ngưỡng chẩn đoán hoặc liều điều trị cho từng bà mẹ, trẻ em.


### Vì sao giai đoạn 1.000 ngày đầu đời là trọng tâm?


Tài liệu mô tả thiếu dinh dưỡng, thiếu vi chất, thừa cân và béo phì ảnh hưởng sức khỏe, sự phát triển trẻ và gánh nặng kinh tế; can thiệp từ thụ thai đến hai tuổi có ý nghĩa đối với sức khỏe lâu dài. Nguồn đặt vấn đề trong bối cảnh các biện pháp y tế, giáo dục, hệ thống thực phẩm, bảo trợ xã hội và nước sạch – vệ sinh.


### Những nhóm biện pháp nào liên quan trực tiếp đến CareBridge?


Tài liệu đề cập chăm sóc dinh dưỡng cho phụ nữ mang thai và sau sinh; tư vấn chế độ ăn và vận động; dùng sắt và axit folic trong chăm sóc trước sinh theo khuyến nghị phù hợp; hỗ trợ thực hiện **Mười bước nuôi con bằng sữa mẹ thành công** ở cơ sở sản khoa; theo dõi tăng trưởng trẻ thường quy; cung cấp bổ sung hoặc bột đa vi chất cho trẻ **khi cần**; xử trí suy dinh dưỡng cấp và phòng ngừa thừa cân/béo phì. Việc lựa chọn biện pháp phải đúng nhóm tuổi, bối cảnh và hướng dẫn chuyên môn.


### Cách đọc mục tiêu 2030


Đọc bảng mục tiêu và dữ liệu thực trạng trong phần nguyên bản kèm mốc thời gian. Không biến mục tiêu chính sách đến năm 2030 thành kết quả đã đạt vào năm 2026, và không dùng tỷ lệ quần thể để kết luận tình trạng của một người.

Malnutrition has long-term effects on 
health, development and economic 
growth
Malnutrition has short- and long-term effects on the 
health, development and economic growth of individuals, 
communities and nations. It is a major cause of death, 
illness and health inequities, particularly among children 
under five years of age, with almost half of child deaths 
linked to undernutrition (1). It also creates cycles of 
poverty by reducing workforce productivity and increasing 
healthcare costs. Ensuring optimal nutrition, especially 
during the first 1000 days from conception to two years

of age, is essential for long-term health and economic 
benefits (1). Tackling malnutrition in all its forms, 
including undernutrition (wasting, stunting, underweight), 
micronutrient deficiencies, overweight, obesity, and 
resulting diet-related noncommunicable diseases, requires 
a coordinated effort from key sectors such as education, 
food and agriculture, health and nutrition, and social 
protection systems, and water, sanitation and hygiene 
(WASH). By increasing education, transforming food 
systems, expanding health and nutrition services, 
strengthening social protection, and implementing WASH 
policies, we can ensure better nutrition for all, leaving no 
one behind.
This brief outlines the rationale for extending the 2025 Global Nutrition Targets to 2030 and introduces 
the extended and new operational targets. It provides an overview of nutrition interventions that 
should be implemented at scale in order to drive progress in maternal, infant, and young child 
nutrition. The World Health Organization (WHO) and UNICEF have developed six content‑specific 
briefs, each addressing a respective target in detail and outlining the actions required to achieve it.

Malnutrition remains a global 
challenge
Despite the progress made in recent decades, maternal, 
infant, and young child malnutrition remains a global 
issue (see Fig. 1). Conflict, climate change, poverty and 
gender discrimination are among the major drivers of 
malnutrition. While the number of people facing hunger is 
on the rise due to ongoing conflicts and global economic 
challenges (2), at the same time, cheap ultra-processed 
foods that are high in energy, fats, free sugars, and salt, 
are being aggressively marketed to children. These factors, 
combined with reduced levels of physical activity, are 
fueling the obesity crisis. The nutrition transition (3) and 
changes in our food environments have contributed to 
one out of every eight people in the world living with 
obesity, as of 2022 (4). To add to the complexity, many 
low- and middle-income countries face a double or triple 
burden of malnutrition, with concurrent undernutrition, 
micronutrient deficiencies and rising obesity rates (4, 5). 
Malnutrition is not confined to low-income countries; 
high-income countries also grapple with increasing obesity 
rates and micronutrient deficiencies (2).
Progress and gaps are evident
Notable successes in addressing malnutrition can be 
cited. The global rate of exclusive breastfeeding amongst 
infants less than six months of age increased from 37% in 
2012 to 48% in 2024 and is expected to reach the target 
of 50% by 2025 (6), while the prevalence of overweight 
in children under five years of age have remained stable, 
indicating that the 2025 target is close to being met. (5). 
These achievements should be celebrated, and the lessons 
learned applied to scaling up other proven interventions. 
Despite some progress, however, we are unlikely to meet 
all the 2025 Global Nutrition Targets. While there has 
been a notable decline in stunting and to a lesser extent 
in wasting among children under five years of age, this is 
insufficient to meet the 2025 goals (5). No progress has 
been made in reducing the prevalence of low birth weight, 
which remains stagnant at nearly 15% (2). As of 2023, the 
prevalence of anaemia in women 15-49 years of age has 
also remained stagnant at 30.7% compared to the 2012 
baseline of 27.6%.
The economic benefits of scaling up nutrition investments 
far outweigh the costs and offer substantial returns on 
investment. The World Bank’s 2024 Investment framework 
estimates that every dollar spent on early nutrition 
interventions can yield US$ 23 in return, contributing 
up to US$ 2.4 trillion in economic benefits (8). However, 
significant investments are needed, with an additional 
US$ 128 billion required over the next decade to scale up 
the needed nutrition interventions.

Extending the targets to 2030
In 2019, UNICEF and WHO published a discussion paper on 
extending the timeline of the targets to align with the 2030 
Sustainable Development Goals (9). To further build on this 
discussion WHO conducted an online public consultation 
in 2024 suggesting new targets and proposing the 
introduction of a number of process-related operational 
targets (10). During the 78th World Health Assembly in 
2025, WHO’s Member States passed a resolution to extend 
the target for five more years to 2030, during which 
time actors can intensify attention to, investment in, 
and actions to address the targets (11). For targets that 
are close to being reached, such as those for exclusive 
breastfeeding and overweight in children under five years 
of age, the focus has shifted to accelerating progress, 
aiming to increase exclusive breastfeeding amongst infants 
under six months of age to 60% and reduce overweight to 
less than 5% in children under five years of age by 2030. 
To increase efforts and measure progress, WHO 
recommends adopting operational targets to measure 
key indicators that are proximally linked to the 2030 
Nutrition Targets. The relationships between these 
process indicators and the nutritional outcome targets are 
illustrated in Fig. 2. Details for each of the indicators are 
provided in Table 1. 
The extended global nutrition targets to be achieved by 
2030 are:
1 A 40% reduction in the number of children under five 
years of age who are stunted, compared to the 2012 
baseline.
2 A 50% reduction in anaemia in women of reproductive 
age, compared to the 2012 baseline.
3 A 30% reduction in low birth weight, compared to the 
2012 baseline.
4 Reduce and maintain overweight in children under 
five years of age to less than 5%.
5 Increase the rate of exclusive breastfeeding in the first 
six months up to at least 60%.
6 Reduce and maintain wasting in children under five 
years of age to less than 5%.

Global efforts are in place to tackle 
malnutrition
Since the launch of the Comprehensive implementation 
plan on maternal, infant and young child nutrition (12), 
there have been numerous impactful global efforts to 
combat malnutrition:
·         The Nutrition for Growth (N4G) (13) summits have 
raised billions of dollars and mobilized hundreds of 
policy and programmatic commitments to reduce 
malnutrition.
·         The Second International Conference on Nutrition 
(ICN2) (14) established a Framework for Action in 2014 
that was endorsed by over 170 governments, setting 
a global agenda for food systems and nutrition for the 
ensuing decade.
·         New financing mechanisms for nutrition were 
launched, including the Global Financing Facility, 
Power of Nutrition, UNITLIFE, Nutrition impact bonds, 
and more recently the Child Nutrition Fund.
·         The UN Decade of Action on Nutrition (2016-2030)
(15) co-led by the Food and Agriculture Organization of 
the United Nations (FAO) and WHO, the Decade calls 
for multisectoral efforts to achieve the global nutrition 
targets by 2030 across six action areas:
– sustainable, resilient food systems for healthy diets;
– aligned health systems providing universal coverage 
of essential nutrition actions;
– social protection and nutrition education;
– trade and investment for improved nutrition; 
– safe and supportive environments for nutrition at 
all ages; and
– strengthened governance and accountability for 
nutrition.
·         The UN Food Systems Summit (UNFSS) (16) held in 
2021 brought together diverse global stakeholders 
to advance progress towards the Sustainable 
Development Goals, focusing on sustainable food 
access, consumption, and production. UNFSS conducts 
a stocktaking moment every two years to review 
progress on the commitments made. 
·         The Committee on World Food Security (17) holds 
annual sessions to develop policies for eliminating 
hunger and malnutrition, with the 2024-2027 strategy 
focusing on resilient livelihoods, food security, 
environmental sustainability, and gender equality.
·         The Scaling Up Nutrition Movement (SUN) (18)
brings together civil society, donors, UN agencies, 
businesses and researchers aims to support countryled, multisectoral strategies to address all forms of 
malnutrition. 
·         Three alliances were formed by WHO, UNICEF and 
partners.
– The Global Breastfeeding Collective (19) equips 
partners with tools ranging from communication 
resources, advocacy briefs, and implementation 
guidance to strengthen regional and local advocacy 
on breastfeeding.
– The Global Complementary Feeding Collective (20)
calls on governments, policymakers and civil 
society to take concrete, coordinated and 
synergistic actions to accelerate access to 
nutritious, safe and diverse diets during the 
complementary feeding period. 
– The Anaemia Action Alliance (21). Under the 
leadership of WHO and UNICEF, the Alliance was 
created to foster accelerated and coordinated 
efforts among all actors and stakeholders. The 
Alliance focuses on workstreams for national 
integrated action, programme implementation, 
investment, and research.
·         Two global action plans were formed.
– The Global Action Plan (GAP) on Child Wasting (22), 
led by FAO, UNHCR, UNICEF, WFP and WHO aims to 
stimulate and accelerate progress towards reducing 
child wasting.
– The WHO acceleration plan to stop obesity (23)
promotes the development of comprehensive 
policies on infant and young child feeding, healthy 
diets, physical activity, and health promoting 
schools, and calls for integrating obesity prevention 
and treatment into primary health care services and 
school health programmes.

Ending malnutrition in all its forms 
requires a multisectoral approach 
Ending malnutrition in all its forms requires integrating 
nutrition into all sectors, including education, food and 
agriculture, health and nutrition, and social protection 
systems, and WASH. Food systems must be transformed in 
order to offer affordable, safe, nutritious, and sustainable 
diets (24, 25, 26). Nutrition services should be fully 
integrated into health systems to improve maternal, child, 
adolescent and adult health (24, 27, 28). By redesigning 
cash and food transfers to promote healthy diets and 
preventive care, social safety nets can help break the cycle 
of poverty and malnutrition. Strong WASH policies reduce 
child malnutrition and prevent diseases such as diarrhoea 
(24). Recognizing that the six Global Nutrition Targets are 
interlinked (see Fig. 2), many of the priority interventions 
described in each of the briefs can advance multiple 
targets. Key interventions to implement across sectors are 
highlighted in Table 2.
