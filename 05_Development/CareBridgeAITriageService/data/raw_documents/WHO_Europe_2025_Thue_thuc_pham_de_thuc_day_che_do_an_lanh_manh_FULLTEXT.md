This policy brief provides an evidence-informed overview of health taxes on food as an effective fiscal policy to create
healthier food environments, promote healthier diets and reduce the burden of diet-related noncommunicable
diseases. The document outlines various types of food taxes and examines their practical implications for policy
development and implementation. The document also highlights key principles of good practice to guide policymakers
in designing equitable and effective health tax policies.

Background
The rationale for health taxes on food
Unhealthy diets are a leading cause of growing rates of obesity and
noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) worldwide. This results from the
increasing availability, marketing and intakes of foods that typically do not
contribute to a healthy diet, such as foods high in saturated fat, salt, sugar
and calories. In the WHO European Region, dietary risks and high body mass
index account for about 11% of the disease burden each, with dietary risks
accounting for 18.5% of deaths (1). Diet-attributable deaths from
cardiovascular diseases increased by 43.8% from 1990 to 2019 globally (2).
Current levels of overweight and obesity mean income losses equivalent to
3.5% of gross domestic product (GDP) in G20 countries, according to the
Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) (3).
Large-scale production and international trade have resulted in a steady
increase in the global availability of highly processed industrially produced
foods, boosted by pervasive brand marketing. These foods are usually
cheaper, per unit of energy, and readily available to consumers. On the other
hand, fresh and healthier foods have become relatively more expensive over
time, and a healthy diet remains unaffordable for millions including in the
European Region, according to the Food and Agriculture Organization of the
United Nations (FAO) (4).
Food systems, from production to consumption, also have far-reaching
environmental costs and account for over one third of global greenhouse gas
(GHG) emissions (5). The FAO estimates the hidden costs arising from
agrifood systems as being equivalent to 10% of global GDP, with an 8.6%
increase in net costs from 2019 to 2023 (6). These hidden costs include
environmental, social and health costs, with the latter calculated as
productivity losses due to obesity and NCDs as a result of food consumption.
The FAO highlights these hidden costs as being “greater than the current
value added of agrifood systems” (7).2
Taxes for healthy eating: what this means in practice
Food taxes to promote healthy diets require a different approach from that
used with products that are more established targets for health taxes, such
as tobacco and alcohol products, despite sharing the same goals of reducing
costs that are not accounted for in consumption choices, such as costs to
others, and costs to the consumer’s future self (in economics: externalities
and internalities). The primary goal of health taxes on tobacco and alcohol
is to discourage consumption. In the case of food, instead, externalities and
internalities are driven by what foods are consumed, in what proportions, and
whether people have access to, and can afford, a healthy and nutritious diet.
The relative prices of different foods play an important role in shaping people’s
food choices. Taxes that are applied differently to different foods, whether
through excises applied to selected foods or through general consumption
taxes, can shift relative prices and incentivize healthier food choices. Most
countries do apply taxes to food and already apply different tax rates to
different foods. Therefore, for those countries, applying health taxes to food
effectively means in the first instance aligning tax rates with health goals. New
taxes may or may not be required.
The purpose of health taxes on food
Food taxes can contribute to promoting healthier diets and population health.
In 2016, WHO convened a technical meeting of global experts in fiscal policies.
The group recommended the use of fiscal policies to incentivize healthier
diets (7) and, at a minimum, “to reduce excessive consumption of less healthy
foods” (8). By 2022, 108 countries had introduced some form of taxes on SSBs
for health improvement purposes (9). Taxes can encourage manufacturers
to reformulate foods, reducing levels of nutrients such as salt, saturated fat
and sugar, which are often consumed in excess. They can also help people
to change their food choices, curbing the demand for unhealthy products.
This is particularly relevant among households with lower incomes, already
at higher risk of unhealthy diets, as those households tend to respond to price
increases caused by tax changes with greater reductions in consumption.
Food taxes can also generate additional revenues for governments, some of
which may be earmarked to address the health consequences of unhealthy
diets, overweight and obesity (10).

Types of food taxes
Most countries tax food as part of their wider fiscal policies, but fewer countries
apply food taxes specifically designed to improve diet quality and health.
Among the latter, perhaps the most prominent examples are Colombia,
Hungary and Mexico (Box 1). Health taxes on foods that typically do not
contribute to a healthy diet are also applied in a number of island states and
smaller jurisdictions, while they have been partly repealed in three northern
European countries (Denmark, Finland and Norway).
Three types of taxes, shown in Fig. 2, are most commonly applied to food and
non-alcoholic beverages. While the majority of countries using taxes to
promote healthy diets apply excise taxes, some use value added or sales
taxes or import duties. Any of those taxes can be used for health improvement
purposes, although each has strengths and limitations (11). Combining a tax
with incentives such as subsidies, or tax reductions, on healthy foods could
alleviate the impact of price increases for low-income households and
enhance health benefits (11).

• In December 2022, Colombia introduced a tax on foods high in salt and saturated fat or

Hungary
• An excise tax was introduced in September 2011 with the aim of promoting healthier food and
beverage choices and encouraging reformulation. Products subject to the tax were chosen
because of the availability of healthier alternatives.
• An NPM was created specifically for the tax with categories based on identified excess nutrient
consumption. It included thresholds for contents of salt, sugars and other specific ingredients.
• Further adjustments to the tax were introduced in subsequent years with the option added

Good practice principles
The process of designing health taxes on food and beverages should always
start from a clear vision of the objectives of the tax. As illustrated in Fig.3, taxes
can be broadly aimed at improving nutritionally imbalanced diets or can be
targeted at increasing or decreasing the consumption of specific nutrients
or food groups. They can be designed to primarily change consumer choices,
or food formulations by manufacturers, or both. They can try to reconcile
health and environmental sustainability goals, or address yet other goals in
food and nutrition policy. In all cases, tax design should follow the good
practice principles set out in this section.
Use a holistic definition of nutritional quality
Current health taxes on food and beverages tend to focus on
a relatively narrow range of foods. For example, Mexico’s tax
on foods with an energy density above 275kcal/100g has a
wider tax base than other taxes, but despite this it only covers
14% of caloric intakes (13). Existing taxes also often focus on a single nutrient
(e.g. sugar) and tend to be small as a proportion of food prices. This narrow
scope means that effects are limited and may be offset by increasing intakes
of nutrients not directly targeted by taxation, which may occur as a result of
food reformulation by manufacturers or food substitutions by consumers.
Health taxes on food should be more broadly based and should take a more
holistic approach in identifying foods to be taxed at higher and lower rates.
A prominent example of a holistic approach is the use of NPMs, increasingly
common in nutrition policies such as public food procurement, front-of-pack
labelling and food advertising regulation (14). There is scope for a wider use
of NPMs as a basis for food taxation, currently limited to a few countries (e.g.
Colombia, Hungary). NPM-based food taxes can be applied to a wide range
of foods based on their overall nutritional quality, thus preventing unwarranted
reformulations by manufacturers or substitutions by consumers. A growing
interest in NPM-based food taxes is also driven by the possibility that a
common NPM basis may create synergies across policies and enhance policy
impacts. Different policies may require adaptations to the structure or mode
of implementation of a common NPM, but the core principles of the NPM –
establishing which foods are consistent with a healthy diet and which less
so – should ideally be applied across all policies.

Modelling studies have shown that NPM-based food taxes can be more
effective in curbing excess calorie and nutrient intakes than taxes targeting
individual nutrients, especially when combined with subsidies – which can
take the form of tax reductions – on healthier products (15,16).
In many countries, the debate around foods that contribute to unhealthy diets
has been increasingly focused on certain types of industrial processing, and
on the concept of ultra-processed food, introduced by the Nova
classification (17). Foods classified by Nova – and other food classifications
based on aspects of food processing that have been developed after Nova –
as most highly processed tend to have poor nutrient profiles, and some NPMs
(e.g. the Pan American Health Organization NPM) incorporate aspects of
processing in their algorithms. However, food classification approaches
based on processing and those based on nutrients are conceptually different,
and if used individually as a basis for food policies, like taxation, they will lead
to targeting partly different foods. A study comparing Nova and Nutriscore,
the latter being based on an NPM, has shown that ultra-processed foods tend
to have poor Nutriscore ratings, but the two classifications are not completely
aligned, with roughly one in four food products with the best Nutriscore rating
(A) being ultra-processed (18).
Classifications like Nova can contribute to establishing a basis for health taxes
on food; however, governments always need to weigh several elements in
deciding what nutritional quality criteria would best support food taxes and
the goals they are designed to pursue. For instance, a classification based on
industrial processing criteria may provide less scope for reformulation by
manufacturers than one based on nutrient content.
A second consideration is the risk of potential legal challenges to the criteria
adopted as a basis for food taxation. Governments can strengthen their
position against potential legal challenges by ensuring that the taxation
approach: (i) is developed using scientific evidence on health risks; (ii) does
not discriminate between like products; and (iii) is not restrictive of trade any
more than is necessary to protect health.
Start from an assessment of existing food
taxes
Any plan to introduce health taxes on food should start from
a collaborative assessment of the food taxes already in place by public health
and fiscal policy experts and officials. In most instances, this will include
(indirect) taxes on products and services, although in some cases it may
include other taxes, or subsidies, when these may contribute to creating
important price differences between foods (e.g. business rates levied on the
hospitality sector, which may selectively affect the prices of foods consumed
out of home).
210
The first step in this assessment should focus on the size of the overall tax
burden borne by households, which a government may not wish to increase
when designing health taxes on food.
The second step is the mapping of food tax burden to different types of food
products, by nutritional quality and, where relevant, place of production and
consumption. If existing food taxes are poorly aligned with nutritional quality,
there will be scope for redesigning food taxes in ways that would incentivize
healthy eating.
The third step will involve a more detailed assessment of the types of taxes
currently applied to food, the extent to which they can be modified, and the
possible need for new taxes to complement existing ones. This should include
an assessment of applicable domestic laws and customs; trade and
investment agreements to which the state is party, including relevant
restrictions or harmonization requirements; exceptions allowing regulation
to protect public health; and notification provisions.
In designing health taxes on food, governments will want to avoid making
food less affordable for low-income households. In particular, the concern is
about the affordability of a healthy diet, but bearing in mind that the nature
of the food environment in low-income neighbourhoods may make access
to healthy food more difficult, independently of prices.
Incentivize healthy and environmentally
sustainable food choices
Health taxes on food should ideally be aligned with both
health and environmental sustainability goals, as
environmental degradation, in turn, impacts on health.
Food taxes have the potential to simultaneously promote health and mitigate
climate change in high-income, middle-income and some low-income
countries – depending on rates of underweight in the population – if they are
appropriately designed (19). In the United Kingdom, it has been estimated
that incorporating the societal cost of GHG emissions into food prices could
reduce deaths from NCDs by improving nutritional outcomes, while reducing
GHG emissions (20).
A modelling study of possible increases in VAT on meat and dairy products,
and a decrease on fruit and vegetables, in the European Union and United
Kingdom, estimates a 3% reduction in deaths from diet-related diseases, as
well as a 6% reduction in GHG emissions, land use and eutrophication
potential, and a 5% reduction in freshwater use (21). However, health and
environmental goals are not always easily aligned, and trade-offs do exist.

Follow logical steps in the design of health
taxes on food
The design of health taxes on food should follow some logical
steps that require decisions on a number of important
features of the taxes to be implemented. Depending on the
specific needs identified in a given country, a government can decide, for
instance, to adopt a narrowly based tax targeting a relatively small set of
foods that exceed a high NPM threshold. This would mean that the least
healthy foods – those with very poor nutrition profiles – would fall within the
scope of the tax. These foods would likely be scattered across different food
categories and would probably be taxed through an excise tax in addition to
existing consumption taxes. Box 2 outlines some of the administrative
challenges of an NPM-based food tax. Alternatively, the government may opt
for a more broadly based tax, potentially targeting the entire food supply, with
foods taxed at different rates based on one or several NPM thresholds. This
tax could wholly or partially replace existing consumption taxes or provide a
basis to redesign them.

A tax based on an NPM including several nutrients can target a wide range of
foods that do not contribute to a healthy diet, and is less likely to target core
foods recommended in dietary guidelines. However, implementing an NPMbased food tax presents national taxation systems with a few administrative
challenges. In particular, it requires:
Box 2. Administrative challenges of an NPM-based food tax
• capacity by the tax administration to monitor relevant food ingredients and nutrient
contents, or to ensure industry compliance with mandatory reporting requirements, for
example, through mandatory nutrient labelling; and
• capacity to enforce and monitor the correct application of a tax by the industries
concerned – for example, the initial cost of securing industry compliance in implementing
the Danish tax on products high in saturated fat was estimated at 13% of the annual revenue
generated by the tax.
Responses to such administrative challenges may include the following:
• Requiring industry to certify the amounts of nutrients through accredited laboratories would
alleviate the burden on the tax system and encourage product reformulation, thus
increasing compliance.
• Adopting a single approach across policies would reduce both administration and
compliance costs – for example using an NPM-based front-of-pack labelling system would
simplify the determination of the tax rates.

Anticipate and counteract
industry opposition
Most changes in food taxation designed to promote healthy
diets are likely to be met with resistance from certain
stakeholders, particularly from parts of the food, beverage
and hospitality industries whose businesses may be affected by the changes
in taxation. For example, in the European Region, the food and beverage
industry has aggressively opposed the adoption of SSB taxes through
lobbying, threats of legal action, direct funding of research, and peddling of
false and misleading arguments and claims to policy-makers and the wider
public. These actions are generally aimed at preventing or delaying the
implementation of taxation measures, weakening their impact, or pushing
governments to repeal existing policies (22). The public health community
can counteract this opposition by strengthening policy transparency, limiting
corporate influence on health policy-making, exposing industry
misinformation, building strong coalitions between public health experts, and
establishing clear legal frameworks to limit corporate influence. Successful
campaigns have sometimes attributed their success to a collaborative
approach involving a range of organizations and actors, including civil society,
academics, national public health institutions and international organizations.
Design and implement a robust
monitoring system
Monitoring and evaluation of fiscal policies is crucial to their
success. In particular, when the goal of a tax is to create price
incentives to change consumer choices, governments must
ensure that changes in taxation effectively translate into changes in the
prices faced by consumers. Especially, but not exclusively, in the case of tax
reductions, tax passthrough to consumers may be limited in some instances.
A close monitoring of price dynamics and adoption of appropriate measures
to ensure full passthrough is an essential complement to the adoption of
taxation measures to improve diet.
