1.1. Good governance

1.1.1. Overview
Governance is the first objective of the Mental Health Action Plan 2013–20: “to strengthen effective
leadership and governance for mental health.” It goes on to say: “As the ultimate guardian of a
population’s mental health, governments have the lead responsibility to put in place appropriate
institutional, legal, financing and service arrangements to ensure that needs are met and the mental
health of the whole population is promoted.” Mental health needs to be integrated into the packages
of care delivered in primary health care (PHC) envisioned in the national strategy, policies and plans.
This should then lead the other health system building blocks: increasing mental health capacity of
the PHC workforce, provision of essential medicines, better quality information, etc.
Good governance is a fundamental necessity for carrying forward the process of integration of mental
health in PHC. There are countries which have integrated mental health without this guidance, and
others which, despite having a vision to do it, have not. There may be a variety of reasons why some
have not succeeded, such as lack of finance or deteriorating infrastructure, but fundamental to them
all is how the health systems are governed.

1.1.2. Political commitment and ensuring delivery at all levels
Health systems are set up according to the level and orientation of political commitment and
leadership. The ministry of health as well as provincial and district health officials need to be
involved and supportive. Governance is an evolving process through the whole course of reform
from envisioning to accountability. It determines the vision and plan for how health systems should
develop and reform. It makes decisions about finance, and how the system, including service delivery
and management, is organized. There may be an inspired vison and an excellent plan, but failure
to deliver due to lack of governance – good governance ensures that plans are implemented and
sustained through clear systems of monitoring, evaluation, review and accountability.
Governance starts from the top, at the national government level, and components can be delegated to
the provincial and district levels depending on the arrangements in individual countries. Increasingly,
decisions are being decentralised, but in doing so, it is important that levels of accountability are
established, and that devolved budgets are spent according to plan. A crucial responsibility at the
national level is to ensure that mental health is included in the essential package of services in PHC
that will respond to health needs and the burden of disease.

1.1.3. Transparent governance
It is a requirement that governance is transparent. Transparent governance is an important proxy
for the quality of governance. Transparent governance is built on the ethical behaviour of public
servants, who ensure that reliable information on government decisions is made publicly available
and that it can be used to engage the participation of citizens and stakeholders. It is an expression
of the principles of freedom of access to information and freedom of expression, as set out in Article
19 of the UN Universal Declaration of Human Rights, 1948. It exposes corruption that undermines

 1.2. Leadership to manage integration of mental health care in
 primary health care
 Establish clear managerial focal points for mental health services at ministry of health, provincial
 and district levels, with defined roles and responsibilities for planning, implementation, governance,
 coordination and review of mental health care. Ideally, to coordinate this activity, each country should
 have a mental health director at the ministry of health, and at least one mental health professional should
 be appointed in every World Health Organization (WHO) country office. It is envisioned that a national
 implementation unit or a cross-cutting team with membership from the PHC directorate, mental health
 directorate, health sector reform directorate, health management information system (HMIS) and
 quality assurance will be established to oversee the implementation of mental health in PHC (see Fig. 1).
 In countries in stable circumstances, this unit will be established by and responsible to the ministry
 of health, with technical or financial contributions provided by partner organizations. In emergency
 settings, it may be necessary for international partner organizations such as WHO, UNICEF and the
 International Medical Corps (IMC) to work with the government in establishing and supporting the unit.
 The aims of the unit will be to plan, coordinate, implement and review the reform process. Integration
 of mental health into PHC is just one building block of policy – it needs to be positioned in the context
 and time course of the whole health sector strategy, and the whole mental health strategy. A collaborative

2.1. Service organization in the Eastern Mediterranean Region

2.1.1. Implementation
WHO is working on accelerating the adoption of family practice in the countries of the Region. At its
63rd session in October 2016, the WHO Regional Committee for the Eastern Mediterranean adopted
resolution EM/RC63/R.2 on Scaling up family practice: progressing towards universal health coverage.
The resolution urged Member States to incorporate the family practice approach into PHC services as
an overarching strategy to advance towards UHC. The level of political commitment towards improving
service provision through the family practice approach is rising in the countries of the Region.
Recent assessment for the essential health services package, including mental services, of a number of
countries in the Region, shows patchy implementation in most countries. The assessment revealed the
challenges facing implementation: improper health system infrastructure, absence of an integrated
approach, shortage of staff, weak referral systems and feedback mechanisms, lack of private sector
engagement, limited financial support, and poor client satisfaction and community perception on
public facilities.

2.1.2. Challenges facing integration
■ Ministries of health: There is inadequate focus on PHC in most of countries, gaps in delivering the
 essential health service package, limited integrations of mental health services, fragmented district
 health management (also see Section 1. Governance).
■ Private health sector: The recent analysis showed that the proportion of private sector outpatient
 services used by the population in the Region ranges from 33% to 86%. The percentage of private
 sector services used by the poorest quintile ranges between 11% and 81%. Assessments undertaken
 in countries of the Region have found that while a number of laws and regulations exist to regulate
 the private sector, many are outdated and inadequate to effectively address the current challenges
 and problems. A weak regulatory framework coupled with poor enforcement pose serious
 challenges to patients and impede the development of the health system in most countries in the
 Region (see also Section 1.1.5. Engagement with the private health sector).
■ Public primary care facilities: Public PHC facilities provide less than 50% of the outpatient
 services in most of the Region. Most countries do not have enough public facilities to match
 population needs. Physical inaccessibility and insecurity (destructed infrastructure) are prevalent
 in most of the countries in emergency as well as dysfunctional referral systems.
■ Health workforce: Dual practice is a common phenomenon in most countries of the Region.
 There is lack of exposure to mental health at undergraduate level; 93% of the PHC facilities are
 managed by generalized physicians, who do not have any specialization after completing medical
 school. Recent assessment shows that 80% of PHC physicians did not receive clinical training over

3.1. Health workforce situation in the Eastern Mediterranean
 Region
The core resource in providing mental health services is the PHC staff, supported by the specialist
mental health staff. The involvement of PHC staff is an important factor in the success of new mental
health services (13,14).
In order to strengthen health systems to deliver safe, efficient and appropriate services and move towards
UHC, a health workforce is needed that is sufficient in number, balanced in skill mix and distribution,
competent, functioning, and adequately motivated and supported. Research shows that population
health outcomes suffer when health workers are deficient. The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)
provide a vision to ensure a life with dignity for all with ambitious targets. The health workforce underpins
the proposed health goal, with a target (SDG 3.C) to “Substantially increase health financing, and the
recruitment, development, training and retention of the health workforce in developing countries,
especially in least developed countries”. The health workforce has impact beyond SDG 3; it is an integral
part of achieving several SDGs, including Goals 1, 4, 5, 8, 10 and 17.
The health workforce situation in the Eastern Mediterranean Region mimics global trends. Overall
health workforce shortages and challenges are constraining health systems and making it difficult to
achieve the targets set for UHC and the SDGs. The Region faces an overall shortage of qualified health
workers, with suboptimal and imbalanced production and availability. Low production capacities and the
unmanaged migration of health workers are exacerbating the situation in the countries of the Region.
There is, however, a huge variation in the availability of health workers, with very low densities of these
workers in some countries (Fig. 6).
Policies and strategies for health workforce development are generally inadequate in the Region. There
are only five countries (Afghanistan, Islamic Republic of Iran, Somalia, Sudan and Yemen) that have
recently developed comprehensive health workforce strategic plans. Health workforce governance
capacities remain limited in addressing health workforce challenges, with the weak status of health
workforce departments/directorates in ministries of health, lack of skilled health workforce managers
and suboptimal multisectoral collaboration.
Reliable and updated health workforce information is critically lacking in the Region, and this requires
particular attention. The deficiency is not only limited to numbers: information on financing the health
workforce and labour market dynamics is also limited.
The unprecedented magnitude and scale of crises, affecting almost two thirds of countries in the Region,
represent a serious challenge with ramifications for the health workforce. Violence and conflict are
directly affecting health workers and compelling them to leave, with consequent service gaps in numerous
countries. Those who remain are often working under dangerous conditions with shortages of equipment
and supplies. The education of health professionals is also being interrupted in areas of conflict. Attacks

3.3.1 Task sharing 3.
The provision of mental health care through the PHC delivery
channel can only be achieved if there are sufficient numbers of
staff working in PHC equipped with the knowledge and skills to
provide appropriate mental health care. The huge treatment gap
of untreated people with treatable mental health problems is in

knowledge and skills of mental health delivery are often based in
relatively inaccessible hospitals, while the majority of people with

meets priorities. The PHC team has to be well-organized, with
clarity regarding responsibilities and roles, and the appropriate

4.1 Regional approach to medicines and technology
Reliable and sustainable access to quality-assured essential medicines, vaccines, medical devices,
diagnostics and other technologies is an integral component of the health system, without which
UHC is not possible. Medicines and health technologies must be available, affordable, acceptable and
appropriately prescribed and used. WHO is working with countries in the Eastern Mediterranean
Region to help develop appropriate medicine and technology policies and assist in building
national capacities in efficient and effective management of the supply chain. Promotion of good
governance in national regulation and management of medicines with a focus on accountability and
transparency is our major priority.
In low- and middle-income countries of the Region, 20–60% of the national health budget is spent
on medicines and health technologies. However, not everyone in the Region has proper access to
these resources. This can be attributed to lack of clear policies, weak regulatory oversight, limited
public awareness, and improper management, prescribing and use (see Fig. 7). Moreover, the ability
of countries to overcome these challenges using innovation, local production and/or technology
transfer is limited.
Accordingly, the Region is focusing on improving access to essential medicines and technologies
through the following tools and resources:
■ Health technology policies:
 - Develop national medicines and health technology policies following WHO guidelines.
 - Institute the concept of “good governance of medicines” and medical products to improve
 accountability and transparency in governing health technologies.
 - Support countries in instituting health technology assessment as a tool for evidence-informed
 decision-making in health technology investments.
 - Provide guidance on improving access to high priced medicines.
■ Regulatory system strengthening:
 - Apply good regulatory practices frameworks to improve regulation of medical products.
 - Develop institutional development plans for national regulatory authorities using the WHO
 Global Benchmarking Tool (GBT).
■ Access to essential medical products:
 - Apply health technology assessment tools for new and emerging products to assess their
 efficacy and cost–benefit ratio.
 - Ensure efficient life-cycle management of products through application of WHO guidelines on
 proper health technology management.
 - Support countries in self-sufficiency, pre-qualification and quality management of their medical
 products using WHO programmes dedicated for these purposes.
■ Support countries in the implementation of WHO’s Global Action Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance
 through greater awareness of the problem, knowledge gathering and research into new medical
 products.

 In order to address the existing HIS challenges, WHO has been working with countries since 2012 to
 strengthen national HISs. To achieve this, WHO and countries of the Region agreed to adopt a framework
 that is practical, easy to understand and feasible to implement and that covers key areas necessary for
 health policy development and monitoring. Both WHO and countries in the Region agreed on three
 broad areas that require action and monitoring as a strategy to improve health outcomes. These areas
 are: monitoring health risks and determinants, assessing health status, including morbidity and cause-
 specific mortality, and evaluating health system performance (31). Agreement on these led to a focused
 approach on the key components of a national HIS, and identification of 68 core health indicators that
 need to be monitored under each component.
 In addition to these initiatives, WHO conducted and HIS rapid assessment in 2013, followed by
 comprehensive HIS assessments in selected countries since 2016.4 Broadly, findings from the HIS
 assessments have shown that the Region lags behind in both technological and institutional dimensions.
 The systems have not capitalized on the opportunities that have come with technological advancements
 such as with Internet, cloud, and mobile computing. Consequently, the existing systems are largely
 manual or standalone, in which data cannot be circulated and shared electronically across the health
 system. Institutionally, assessment results showed broadly fragmented systems; non-standardized data
 and data flows that are not being shared; weak human resources capacity; and very limited evidence of data
 being used to inform action. With respect to fragmentation, most national HIS typically included data for
 only some of the health programmes (typically maternal and child health) whereas other programmes
 such as TB and HIV maintained their own systems, resulting in fragmentation. Data on other health
 systems components, such as human resources, finances, and infrastructure, are typically not part of the
 national HIS. The observed fragmentation was occurring across ministries, especially in the case of civil
 registration and vital statistics systems, where data on births and deaths were being collected by the health
 department and the registration of these vital events was the responsibility of another ministry (justice
 or interior). Similarly, data collected through population-based surveys by the statistics departments and
 private sector information were often not easily available to the HIS.
